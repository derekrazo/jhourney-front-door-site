“I try so hard and nothing happens”

You sit down determined to get it right, and the harder you work, the tighter and emptier it feels.

Likely cause

Over-efforting

This is almost certainly the most common pattern we see in beginners. If you worked hard for most of what you have, effort is your default tool, and here it backfires: effort adds the very tension the practice is trying to release. Enjoyment, curiosity and relaxation are more about not-doing than doing, and most of us have to learn how to relax rather than assume we already know.

M1 Usually shows up at Finding a spark on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

20 min

Cut it in half. Then again.

  1. Before you sit, notice how much effort you’re planning to bring.
  2. Take whatever effort you’re planning to apply, and cut it in half. Then, do so again.
  3. Every few minutes, ask: how could I do this with half as much effort? Then try 10× less.
  4. Coach yourself like a team of 8-year-old soccer players, not like a new batch of marines. Praise, warmth, no drill sergeant.
  5. At the end, check three things: are you more relaxed, in a better mood, and softer or more curious than when you started? If not, effort probably crept back in.

Experiment 2

10 min

Ways of smiling

  1. Smile for one minute. Notice how you feel.
  2. Smile for one minute at half the effort. Then half of that.
  3. Smile with the least effort you can manage.
  4. Try smiling with your eyes, then with your heart, then with your whole body.
  5. Keep whichever smile felt best, and rest your attention on the feeling it makes, not on the smile.

From the fieldOne teacher we admire says there are only two rules on retreat: smile and have fun.

When to stopIf sits leave you with a headache, a clenched jaw or a tight chest, stop for the day and rest. “Powering through” isn’t good practice. It’s careless. How to regulate

“My mind won’t stop talking”

Plans, replays and to-do lists keep pulling you away, and every one feels like a small failure.

Likely cause

Treating distractions as the enemy

Mind-wandering isn’t the problem; how you meet it is. A useful assumption is that every distraction points to some tension that wants your attention. Waking up from one is your chance to relax that tension and welcome that part of you into the sit. You make progress each time a distraction ends in gratitude and relaxation, and lose ground each time it ends in suppression.

M3 Usually shows up at Settling in on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

15 min

Thank it, relax something, return

  1. Start with your warm feeling, however faint.
  2. When you notice you’ve wandered, pause and briefly thank whatever pulled you. Worry is trying to protect you. Boredom is looking for something that matters.
  3. Relax one thing in your body: jaw, shoulders or hands. It doesn’t need to be related to the thought.
  4. Return gently to the feeling, as if a friend just arrived late and you’re glad they came.
  5. Don’t keep score. Each return is one rep, and it speeds up with practice.

Experiment 2

5 min

Do Nothing first

  1. Before your main sit, let attention roam with no agenda.
  2. Whenever you notice an intention to focus, sort something out or plan, drop it as soon as you recognise it.
  3. After five minutes, start the basic technique from that looser place.

From the fieldBeginners often report only 1–3 mind-wanderings an hour. Later they notice one every 30 seconds to 3 minutes. That isn’t getting worse. It means their awareness has sharpened.

When to stopIf your thoughts are racing and your heart or chest joins in, that’s hyperarousal, not a technique problem. Tap the brakes: open your eyes, widen your attention to the room, or go for a walk. How to regulate

“I get sleepy or foggy”

You nod off, or sits drift into a comfortable haze where time passes and nothing builds.

Likely cause

Sleep debt, or spacing out mistaken for calm

Often it’s plain tiredness. Most people arrive at a retreat short on sleep, and meditating through heavy drowsiness doesn’t lead anywhere useful. The subtler version is drifting into a pleasant blank and calling it relaxation. Real calm feels connected and clear. Spacing out feels absent.

M3 Usually shows up at Settling in on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

20–40 min

Nap first, then sit

  1. If you keep nodding off, lie down and take a nap. It’s not a failure.
  2. When you wake, sit straight away and use the fresh, clear feeling after a nap.
  3. If you’re behind on sleep, make sleep the priority for a few days before judging your practice.

Experiment 2

10 min

Did I know what would happen?

  1. Sit a little more upright. Eyes can be slightly open.
  2. Every minute or so, check in and ask: did I know what was going to happen? What happens next?
  3. Get interested in the texture of the feeling: its shape, temperature and movement.
  4. If the fog rolls back in, open your eyes fully and look around before going back in.

From the fieldFrom a facilitator’s debrief notes: “Curiosity is energizing. If I am feeling sleepy I can do a practice like this.”

When to stopIf you feel numb, far away or unreal rather than sleepy, that’s dissociation. Apply gas: move, open your eyes, talk to someone. If you’re still sleeping all the time after you’ve caught up, something else may be going on, and that comes first. How to regulate

“I don’t feel anything pleasant at all”

You do the phrases or the smile, and wait, and nothing feels warm or good.

Likely cause

Looking for fireworks

Most people expect a big, obvious feeling. What you’re looking for at first is small: a slight warmth, a softening, a hint of gladness. We learn to be overwhelmed by how we relate to the feeling, not by the feeling becoming huge. If joy feels out of reach, gratitude is often easier to find.

M1 Usually shows up at Finding a spark on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

10 min

Turn to gratitude

  1. Look for gratitude for someone in your life.
  2. Then for something about yourself. Then for how you’ve learned and grown. Then for basic conditions, like a roof or a breath.
  3. Notice which was easiest, and stay there.
  4. Let go of the specific thing and rest in the feeling of gratitude itself. Can you feel grateful for gratitude?

Experiment 2

45 min

Audition your scaffolding

  1. Pick three: a smile, a warm memory (a loose sense is fine, like the feel of a puppy), wishing a loved one well, or a phrase like “May I be at ease.”
  2. Give each one at least 15 minutes. Use it gently, every 5–30 seconds, and watch what follows.
  3. Rate each by the faintest openhearted feeling it produced.
  4. Keep the winner for tomorrow. Something that works even 20% of the time counts.

From the field“Can’t feel emotions in the body at all” is one of the starting points listed in our retreat booklet. It’s where some people begin, not a verdict.

In the vaultWays of Smiling, the Gratitude sit, and the Scaffolding recordings for memories and visualisation.

“I get tingles or buzzing, but it doesn’t go anywhere”

There are pleasant physical sensations, but no warmth, joy or emotion in them.

Likely cause

Over-focusing on physical sensations

Jhanas are deeply emotional states. Maybe 1 in 10 students we see focus only on body sensations, often because they’ve heard jhana starts with a “body buzz.” Stay in the body, but look for the emotion there. Not every body sensation has an emotional side, but every emotion has a body side, and that’s where to rest your attention.

M2 Usually shows up at Keeping the spark on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

15 min

What’s pleasant about the pleasantness?

  1. Find the pleasant sensation.
  2. Ask, gently: what is pleasant about this pleasantness?
  3. Look for anything emotional inside it: gladness, tenderness, friendliness, relief.
  4. When you find even a trace, make that your object and let the tingle fade into the background.

Experiment 2

10 min

Your body as a heat map

  1. Call up a warm memory or someone you love.
  2. Ask: how do I know I’m feeling this? Where in my body is it?
  3. Find the most intense part. Does it have a shape or a location?
  4. Sink into that spot as if nothing else existed.

From the fieldThis is a feeling meditation. It doesn’t mean ignoring the body: the first four jhanas are profoundly in-the-body experiences.

In the vaultThe two Embodied Emotions recordings (emotional inquiry and emotional priming, each with a body scan).

“What worked yesterday doesn’t work today”

Your phrase, memory or image used to spark something. Now it feels dry, so you’re tempted to switch.

Likely cause

Bouncing around

When scaffolding is new, the novelty makes you curious and open without trying. Once it wears off, you start relating to it in a drier way without noticing. The scaffolding didn’t break; your relationship to it changed. That’s a how-to-relate problem, not a which-scaffolding problem, and spotting the difference is the lesson.

M2 Usually shows up at Keeping the spark on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

20 min

Same phrase, new relationship

  1. Use yesterday’s scaffolding, unchanged.
  2. Use it once, then rest and watch for several seconds.
  3. Before using it again, look for anything you’re adding: checking, pushing, expecting.
  4. Try one change at a time: 5% more relaxed, more curious, or savouring it like slipping into a hot tub.
  5. Write down which change helped. That’s tomorrow’s starting point.

Experiment 2

10 min

Phrases, words, no letters

  1. Say the full phrases slowly: “May I be safe. May I be at ease. May I be happy.”
  2. After a few minutes, shorten them to single words: safety, ease, happy.
  3. Then drop the words and just intend the meaning, with no letters at all.
  4. Notice which version feels freshest.

From the fieldDon’t drop your scaffolding when it’s no longer necessary. Drop it when it’s clearly no longer useful, when using it pulls you away from the feeling.

In the vaultSensitivity with Mantras, and a 78-minute “Imitate an expert” sit where Stephen talks through his own moves with mantras.

“The good feeling fades as soon as I notice it”

A warm pulse shows up, then drains away, and you scramble to get it back.

Likely cause

Clinging as it fades

Grabbing at a feeling as it fades adds tension, and tension drains it faster. Early on, fading after each pulse is normal; it’s a stage, not a fault. Treat the fade as relaxation arising. Let the pulse overwhelm you, then relax deeply as it fades, like the glide after a swimming stroke.

M2 Usually shows up at Keeping the spark on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

15 min

Pulse and glide

  1. Use your scaffolding once, as gently as you can.
  2. Watch the feeling for several seconds.
  3. As it fades, don’t chase it. Enjoy the fading as much as the pulse.
  4. Pulse again only after 5–30 seconds.
  5. Over the sit, notice whether the glide gets longer.

Experiment 2

10 min

Smile in, relax out

  1. Breathe naturally.
  2. On the in-breath, smile. On the out-breath, relax.
  3. After five minutes, stop doing anything for a moment.
  4. Then swap: relax on the in-breath, smile on the out-breath.
  5. See if you can find the relaxation in the smile, and the smile in the relaxation.

From the fieldA facilitator on the smile-and-relax exercise: “This could be your whole practice. I went three days on retreat basically only doing this practice.”

In the vaultSinking into Enjoyment, Leaking Enjoyment and Waves of Enjoyment, plus the Fueling Positivity drill.

“It’s pleasant, but flat”

Sits are calm and nice, sometimes for a long time, but nothing deepens.

Likely cause

Assuming you already know the feeling

Once you think you know what the feeling is and how it unfolds, curiosity switches off, and depth goes with it. Curiosity, enjoyment and relaxation can be practised to virtuoso levels; treat them as brand-new skills. One check: real calm feels connected and alive. A pleasant blank where time disappears may be spacing out instead.

M3 Usually shows up at Settling in on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

15 min

Ask a different question

  1. Settle into the feeling.
  2. Ask about qualities you never check: texture, flavour, shape, colour, frequency, depth, width.
  3. You don’t need an answer. Notice what each question does.
  4. Then ask: what would it feel like to turn off expectations? What subtle part of this am I missing?

Experiment 2

10 min

Enjoy the enjoying

  1. Find a small pleasant feeling.
  2. Enjoy it.
  3. Notice that you’re enjoying it, and enjoy that.
  4. Do the same with relaxation: relax into the relaxation, one layer at a time.

From the fieldAmong past participants who reached jhana, 47% said their best moment was as good as or better than the best thing in the past year or more (278 of 595). There’s usually more depth available than a pleasant hour suggests.

When to stopIf “flat” really means numb, foggy or far away, that’s a slow slide into dissociation, not calm. Apply gas: open your eyes, move, or widen your attention to the sounds in the room. How to regulate

“I keep yanking my attention back”

You’re good at staying on the object, but sits get tighter, not looser, as they go on.

Likely cause

Suppressing distractions

This is common among experienced meditators, especially those from Goenka- or IMS-style vipassana or The Mind Illuminated. Pulling attention back by force adds a little tension each time. Relaxing back adds ease. The difference is subtle, but if you’re not getting more relaxed as the sit goes on, you’re almost certainly doing this.

M4 Usually shows up at Finding flow on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

10 min

Drop it gently

  1. Begin without a meditation object.
  2. Notice each sound or thought your attention drifts to.
  3. Drop it as quickly, as gently and as relaxingly as you can.
  4. After ten minutes, start the basic technique and keep returning the same way.

Experiment 2

15 min

Embracing or bracing?

  1. Start with a smile and some relaxation.
  2. Every few minutes, ask: how much am I embracing this, and how much am I bracing against it?
  3. Move slightly toward embracing.
  4. At the end, compare how you feel with how you felt at the start.

From the fieldOne of the most experienced meditators on a past retreat, who had been in jhana years before, did this for days before spotting it. He called it “sweeping distractions under the rug.”

In the vaultEmbracing v Bracing, the Embracing Distractions recordings, and the Pragmatic Guide’s section on suppressing distractions.

“I keep waiting for something to happen”

Part of you is always checking: is this it? Is it working? Am I close?

Likely cause

Jhana or bust

Expectations have an unusually large effect on what you can reach in meditation. Holding the goal tightly creates the tension that keeps it away: constant self-monitoring, no surrender, no jhana. Keep the direction, and let go of the scorecard.

M4 Usually shows up at Finding flow on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

5 min

Name the expectation

  1. Say each phrase slowly, a few times: “This will be playful and fun.” “It is easy for me to feel joy.” “It is easy for me to be relaxed.”
  2. Notice what your mind and body do with each one. Doubt, tightness and a flicker of hope all count.
  3. Name what you find. Naming an expectation can weaken it.

Experiment 2

20 min

A mystery novel

  1. Before you sit, take on one view: “Experience is a mystery novel. I have no clue what the next page says.”
  2. Sit as usual.
  3. Whenever you catch yourself checking for jhana, ask: did I know what was going to happen next?
  4. Afterwards, note whether wonder went up and whether things settled without forcing.

From the fieldOn our retreats the median first jhana came on Day 3, and 43% of the people who got there did so on Day 4 or later (n = 619). Most people need time, not more checking.

In the vaultRelaxing Expectations, the three Patience drills (Finding Comfort, Waiting for the Smile, What Am I Missing), and Not Knowing.

“I get close, but it never tips over”

The feeling builds, something big seems near, and then it plateaus or slips away.

Likely cause

Bracing instead of surrendering

Right at the edge, many people reach for jhana, start analysing it as it unfolds, or tense up as the feeling gets big. Each of those adds a little tension, and that’s enough to stop the shift. The move is to stop building and let the feeling pull you in: keep a direction, but surrender the outcome.

M4 Usually shows up at Finding flow on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

20 min

Let it be bigger than you

  1. When the feeling is building, stop using scaffolding for a while.
  2. Ask: what would it feel like to let this grow so large it overwhelms me?
  3. Set aside everything you know about what comes next. Meet it the way a newborn would.
  4. If you notice bracing, relax that too, and keep going.

Experiment 2

60–90 min

Stay longer once it’s good

  1. Block 90 minutes, with no timer.
  2. Once you’re genuinely enjoying yourself, ask: do I want another 5–10 minutes? What would make that fun?
  3. Keep asking every so often.
  4. Stop when the honest answer is no.

From the fieldMost meditators report an inflection point around 45 minutes, when staying with the feeling starts to take less effort. Going from 60 to 75 minutes can double the time you spend past that point.

When to stopIf letting go brings fear, a sense of losing control or panic, lean back toward safety: open your eyes and feel your feet. Then come back to the edge in small doses. Never push through fear to get in. How to regulate

“I got there once, and now I can’t get back”

You had a jhana, and now every sit is an attempt to recreate it.

Likely cause

Grasping at the memory

For some people this is the hardest stage. Clinging to what the first jhana felt like adds the tension that blocks it. What got you in was the letting go, not the place you let go into. Go back to relaxed enjoyment of the build-up and let it grow without a target.

M6 Usually shows up at Sometimes jhana on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

60 min

Start from scratch

  1. Sit as if you’d never been in jhana: basic technique, small feeling.
  2. When the memory of that sit shows up, thank it and let it go, like any other distraction.
  3. Give yourself one measure for the sit: “How warmly can I relate to this moment?”
  4. Afterwards, note whether the craving for fireworks dropped.

Experiment 2

20 min

Give it away

  1. When a pleasant feeling arises, imagine gifting it to someone you love.
  2. As more arises, give that away too.
  3. Notice whether the striving softens and the feeling widens.

From the fieldAbout half of past participants who reached jhana on retreat left able to get back in reliably (329 of 647). Getting back is its own skill, separate from getting there.

“Hard emotions come up when I sit”

Sadness, fear, anger or old memories show up, sometimes just as things start to feel good.

Likely cause

Relaxing away from it, not into it

This is common, and often a sign the practice is working: with less stimulation, what’s underneath gets a chance to surface. The trap is using relaxation to make the feeling disappear. That’s running away, and it keeps the block in place. As long as you’re within your window of tolerance, the move is to welcome it. Forgiveness meditation is the tool we reach for most.

M3 Usually shows up at Settling in on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

15 min

Forgiveness, gently

  1. Relax first. If there’s a specific memory, call up just a loose sense of it.
  2. Every so often, repeat softly: “I forgive you for not understanding,” “I forgive myself for not understanding,” or “I’m sorry.”
  3. If nothing specific comes up, repeat the phrases anyway with a soft, open awareness.
  4. Spend some of the time, maybe a fifth, on gratitude or kindness.
  5. If it gets to be too much, stop and use the brakes below.

Experiment 2

10 min

Climb the stack

  1. Notice the feeling and where it sits in your body.
  2. Ask: am I relaxing into this, or away from it?
  3. If you can’t welcome the feeling, welcome your resistance to it. If not that, welcome your frustration about the resistance.
  4. Keep zooming out until you find a layer you can meet with kindness, then work back down.

From the fieldWe’ve interviewed dozens of meditation teachers about safety, and they all agreed loving-kindness is the safest kind of meditation.

When to stopIf your heart races, your breathing gets fast and shallow, or you feel panic, flashbacks or numbness, you’re outside your window of tolerance. Stop meditating. Do something that brings you back: exercise, eat something hearty, get outside, call a friend. If you return, go slower, with forgiveness or loving-kindness. If it doesn’t settle, talk to a professional. How to regulate

“I don’t think this works for people like me”

Too much thinking, not enough experience, not emotional enough, not the type.

Likely cause

Limiting beliefs and doubt

If you don’t believe you can enter a jhana, you most likely won’t, because expectations shape what you can reach. Common versions: “I need thousands of hours,” “I need a traditional teacher,” “I’m not an emotional person.” Doubt is also an opening. If you can spot it, name it and change how you relate to it, you’ve practised the same release that jhana runs on.

M0 Usually shows up at Getting ready on the Jhana Map →

Experiment 1

15 min

List them, then test one

  1. Write down every doubt you have, one per line, starting with “I.”
  2. For each one, write the evidence for and against, the way you might in a CBT exercise.
  3. Turn each into a question you can test. “I can’t feel emotions” becomes “What’s the faintest feeling I can find tonight?”
  4. Pick one to test in your next sit.

Experiment 2

20 min

Come here, dear friend

  1. Sit as usual.
  2. Every time a doubt or a self-critical thought shows up, say inwardly: “Come here, dear friend.”
  3. Relax one thing in your body, then return to the feeling.
  4. Afterwards, note whether you recovered from distractions faster and whether your inner tone softened.

From the field53% of past participants with 0–100 hours of lifetime meditation reached jhana on retreat (289 of 544).

Safety first

Stay inside your window

Your window of tolerance is the range where you feel both safe and alive: alert enough to engage, relaxed enough to learn. Everything on this page assumes you’re inside it. If you drift out, regulating comes first, and it isn’t lost progress. It is the practice.

Too much tap the brakes

Signs

  • Racing thoughts
  • Tight chest, racing heart, or fast, shallow breathing
  • Shaking or restlessness
  • Panic or flashbacks
  • Over-intense focus
  • Emotions swinging hard

What to do

  • Stop the sit or make it shorter
  • Widen and loosen your attention, especially to things outside your body
  • Open your eyes and move them around the room
  • Titrate: keep about 90% of your attention on something that feels safe and 10% on the hard sensation
  • If it’s strong, stop meditating and do something grounding: exercise, eat something hearty, get outside, call a friend

Too little add some gas

Signs

  • Numb, spaced-out or flat
  • Foggy or frozen
  • Emotionally blunted
  • Time feels distorted
  • Mind feels blank
  • Pleasant, but far away or unreal

What to do

  • Move your body
  • Open your eyes
  • Talk to someone. Connection is gas.
  • Shift attention outside the body to something safe or neutral, like sounds in the room
  • Use more words and ideas: describe what you see, or plan your next meal

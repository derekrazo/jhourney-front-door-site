Writing for a named reader

Every piece here started as a brief naming its reader: how familiar they are with jhana, why they'd care, and the situation the piece opens on (site/visuals/AUDIENCES.md). Writers worked from the brief, the reader profile and a vetted fact bank, and never saw the curriculum or the earlier pieces. Each piece was then read cold by a separate agent playing that reader, the lessons went back into the system files, and the writers revised.

The pipeline

  1. Brief. Reader (level, family, angle or objection), the one finding, the template, the next step.
  2. Write. A fresh writer per reader level sees only the brief, AUDIENCES.md, facts.md, the design rules and the template spec.
  3. Render. The same templates as /visuals.
  4. Read cold. A separate agent plays the reader, sees only the images, and flags every word, number or "it" it can't place. Advanced pieces also get a stranger's read.
  5. Fix upstream, then revise. Each lesson goes into the system files first; the writers then revise from the reader's notes.

What the test showed

  1. Comprehension failures ("what loop?", "state of what?", "who is Stephen?") fell from 39 to 24, and unresolved pronouns from 40 to 28. All cold-reader flags fell from 149 to 120. Fresh reviewers always find something, so the change in kind matters more than the count.
  2. Round-2 complaints are mostly about trust, not comprehension: numbers judged by Jhourney's own teachers, different denominators on neighbouring slides (1,436 vs 1,015 vs 973), and claims (no craving, a different everyday mood) with nothing but "people report" behind them.
  3. Interest didn't move: 3.5 out of 5 on average in both rounds. Clarity fixes stop a reader getting lost; they don't make a piece more compelling. The highest scorers in both rounds open on the reader's own situation (flat-success, never-worked) or give them something to try (over-efforting).
  4. The cost of standalone slides is repetition. "Deep pleasure and calm" and "Jhourney's week-long meditation retreats" recur on almost every slide, and readers began calling the middles of 7-slide carousels tedious. Rule 1 needs a lighter touch: re-anchor on the slides likely to be screenshotted (the hook, stat slides), not every slide.
  5. Every number traces to facts.md, the vocabulary check ends at zero warnings, and nothing overflows. The three mechanical gates do their jobs, and writers fit their own copy once they can render.

What round 1 taught the system

  1. Each slide needs its own nouns. Readers flagged "the loop", "the state" and "it" on slides that make sense only after the slide before. The rule now applies per slide, and stat slides say who the percentage is of in the headline. (AUDIENCES.md, rule 1)
  2. Name the category early. A cold reader who met "a state of deep pleasure" with no anchor asked "drug-like? sexual?". New-level hooks now say it comes from meditation. (AUDIENCES.md, rule 2)
  3. Context costs space. The definition and "who we are" lines pushed three frames off the bottom. Templates for New and Curious have to budget about three lines for it. (AUDIENCES.md, rule 7)
  4. Insider words the list missed. "Emotional defaults", "alumni", "re-entry", NPS, "Stephen" and "TWIM" got through round 1. Each now has a plain-words row. (AUDIENCES.md, vocabulary)
  5. The approved definition overclaimed. "Most beginners learn to enter it" contradicts 58.7%. The one-paragraph definition now says "more than half of people, most of them beginners". (AUDIENCES.md, definition stack)
  6. The fact bank was missing counts. Writers couldn't show n for the self-reported rate, online vs in person, or life-changing among those who got in, so they left them out. The counts, the day-by-day curve and who Stephen is are now in. (facts.md)
  7. A template field printed its own name. Snag & shift prints `illustration` under each meter; writers filled it with slugs. Now documented as a visible caption. (README.md)

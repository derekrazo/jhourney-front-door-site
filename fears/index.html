Before you book

The 17 fears people bring to a jhana retreat, answered honestly

On the first night of every retreat, our facilitators hand out a worksheet listing these 17 fears. Every one of them has been said out loud by a past participant. Fears that go unspoken tend to quietly shape a week of practice; once named, they usually get smaller, and many have a real answer. Tick the ones that apply to you.

01

Is this real, and can I trust it?

4
  1. Jhanas sound exaggerated or mystical. I’m afraid they’re not real.

    Fair. Intense joy from sitting still with your eyes closed sounds like something invented to sell retreats. Here’s what we mean, plainly: jhanas are states of absorbing, self-sustaining pleasure that come from a feedback loop between attention and good feeling. Anxiety feeds on itself in the same way; this runs in the other direction. They aren’t new, and they aren’t ours. Buddhist texts mapped them 2,500 years ago, and Christian and Sufi contemplatives describe the same states. Researchers at Harvard and McGill have studies underway. You don’t have to take any of this on faith. By the end of a week you’ll know what happened in your own experience.

    The numbersStaff judged that 843 of 1,436 past participants (59%) reached jhana during their retreat.

    What helpsJoin the free 90-minute “Intro to jhanas” workshop (luma.com/jhourney-intro) and try the first steps before you decide anything.

  2. I’m a skeptical person, and I’d want proof before trusting Jhourney with my time and attention.

    Good. Skeptics tend to do well here, because the method is mostly running experiments and noticing what actually happens. We’d rather earn trust with numbers than ask for it. We track every retreat: who reached jhana, on which day, how they rated the week, and what went wrong. The headline isn’t the kind marketing usually prints. 59% reach jhana in a week, which means about 4 in 10 don’t. We also log adverse effects, which most retreats don’t do. If numbers like these make you more comfortable rather than less, that’s a decent sign you’d fit.

    The numbers59% of 1,436 past participants reached jhana. The median first jhana came on Day 3.

    What helpsOn your call, ask for any number you want to see. If we don’t have it, we’ll say so.

  3. I’m scared this will be too “woo-woo” or cult-like for me.

    Reasonable, given how many meditation scenes have formed around a charismatic leader. We teach this as a secular skill. There’s no guru, no belief system to adopt, and no ritual you have to buy into. Teachers give starting instructions and share what has worked for others, then treat you as the lead scientist of your own practice. You’re encouraged to question the instructions, change the technique and report back. The language is plain: feelings in the body, relaxation, curiosity. In-person retreats may include extras such as yoga or breathwork. If one isn’t for you, skip it.

    What helpsBring your most skeptical question to the call and see how we answer it.

  4. I’m scared this won’t be “woo” enough for me. Meditation is sacred, or shouldn’t be taught outside a lineage, monastery or Dharma context.

    This is a serious objection, and we don’t want to wave it away. Many people meet meditation inside a lineage, with ethics, a teacher relationship and a path, and feel something is lost when it’s taught as a skill. We’ve chosen a narrower job: helping people have these experiences reliably and use them in daily life. We don’t teach morality or Buddhist metaphysics. Our teachers have been shaped by Buddhist teaching, and the suttas remain some of the best practice instructions there are. If a lineage context matters to you, a traditional retreat may suit you better, and we’re glad to point you to one.

    What helpsEmail retreats@jhourney.io and ask for a referral to a traditional teacher.

02

Will it work for me?

5
  1. I’m worried Jhourney’s approach to meditation won’t work for me.

    It might not, and we’d rather say so up front: about 4 in 10 people don’t reach jhana in a week. What we can do is explain how the approach differs, so you can judge for yourself. Most meditation trains neutral attention, often on the breath. We start with a pleasant feeling in the body, such as warmth, gratitude or kindness, and relax into it. It’s closer to a feeling practice than a focus practice. People who struggled with breath-focused meditation often find it suits them better. The technique isn’t fixed, either. Your 1:1s with a facilitator are where it gets adjusted to you.

    The numbersResults are close across formats: 57% reached jhana online (n=757) and 60% in person (n=679).

    What helpsTry the free 7-day challenge (15–20 minutes a day) at jhourney.io/resources/7-day-challenge. It uses the same starting technique, so you’ll learn quickly whether it suits you.

  2. It’s not possible for me to learn the jhanas.

    Plenty of people arrive sure of this, and some are right, at least for one week: about 4 in 10 don’t reach jhana on a retreat. But nobody can tell in advance who those people will be, you included. We’ve tried to predict outcomes from pre-retreat questionnaires, and our best attempts barely beat guessing. The belief itself is often the obstacle, because trying hard to prove it wrong creates tension. One question is worth asking: what would it cost you to set the belief aside until the end of the week?

    The numbersAmong complete or near beginners (100 hours of practice or less), 51% reached jhana.

    What helpsIf you can, build a short daily practice now. People who already meditated daily reached jhana more often (71% vs 51%), though that’s a correlation, not a guarantee.

  3. I’m not spiritual or disciplined enough to benefit from a retreat.

    You don’t need to be either. The method is secular and works with ordinary sensations and feelings, so there’s nothing to believe. Discipline matters less than you’d think, and too much of it backfires. The most common mistake we see is over-efforting: treating jhana as a feat of focus and trying to force it. The instruction you’ll hear most is some version of relax, enjoy and do less. You’re encouraged to sleep well, and to nap instead of meditating when you’re tired. What helps most is curiosity about your own experience and a willingness to run small experiments.

    What helpsTry a few NSDR (non-sleep deep rest) recordings on YouTube. They build the relax-and-feel skill without encouraging strain.

  4. If I don’t learn the jhanas on a retreat, it will have been a waste of my time, or I’ll have failed.

    This is one of the most common fears, and we take it seriously, because it can become the reason someone doesn’t get there: gripping the outcome adds tension. The honest numbers: people who don’t reach jhana rate the retreat lower, with an NPS of 47 against 82 for those who do. But 36% of them still call it life-changing. What they usually describe is the work that comes before jhana: feeling emotions more fully, replacing an inner critic with something kinder, and learning to practise without striving. Those skills stay with you even if jhana takes longer.

    The numbers“Life-changing”: 64% of those who reached jhana said so, and 36% of those who didn’t.

    What helpsBefore you go, write down what would make the week worthwhile apart from jhana. Our facilitators suggest this to everyone who books.

  5. I already have an extensive meditation background. I’m worried I won’t learn much.

    Possibly true, and worth checking. If you already enter jhana reliably and move through the later ones, a beginner-friendly week may teach you less than a longer, advanced retreat. But experience usually helps here: in our data, people with 1,000 to 10,000 hours of practice reached jhana 70% of the time, against 53% for those with under 100. The usual snag for experienced meditators is the approach. Many traditions train neutral observation; we ask you to get involved, to enjoy a pleasant feeling and let it grow. That can feel wrong at first. Experienced practitioners often use the week to build reliable access and explore the later jhanas.

    The numbersReached jhana: 53% with up to 100 hours of practice, 60% with 100–1,000 hours, 70% with 1,000–10,000 hours (n=1,305).

    What helpsOn your call, describe your practice and ask straight out whether a retreat would teach you something new. If it won’t, we’ll tell you.

03

The people in the room

4
  1. Other participants will be more advanced, and I’ll feel out of place.

    A common worry, and it usually doesn’t hold up. Retreats are built for beginners, plenty of participants have never meditated before, and everyone works on their own practice at their own pace. Experience does help on average, but less than people assume. Across past cohorts, retreats with a larger share of beginners did as well as or better than the rest. Your 1:1s are about where you are, not where anyone else is.

    The numbers51% of participants with 100 hours of practice or less reached jhana, against 59% overall.

  2. I’d feel in competition with other participants to make progress.

    Very normal. Put a group of motivated people in a room with the same goal and comparison follows. It’s worth noticing early, because comparison produces exactly the striving that slows this practice down. It also misleads you. The median first jhana comes on Day 3, but the range is wide: about 40% of the people who get there do so on Day 4 or later. Someone else’s early breakthrough tells you very little about your own week. And since retreats are mostly silent, you’ll hear less about other people’s progress than you might expect.

    The numbersAmong people who reached jhana, about 57% had by Day 3 and 92% by Day 6.

    What helpsWhen you catch yourself comparing, treat it as data. Notice where the tension sits in your body. That counts as practice too.

  3. I’d know other people on the retreat, and that would make it uncomfortable to share my experiences candidly.

    Understandable. It’s hard to be honest about a tearful sit, or about three days of nothing happening, when a colleague is down the hall. A few things help. Retreats are mostly silent, so there’s little small talk. The most candid conversations happen in private 1:1s with your facilitator, not in front of the group. In group sessions you share as much or as little as you like. If it still bothers you, choose a different date or format from the people you know.

    What helpsMention it on your call. We can suggest a retreat where you’ll know fewer people.

  4. I’m worried I won’t get the support I want from the Jhourney team, or that we have different goals.

    Fair to ask before you pay. Support comes in three forms: morning and evening group sessions with instruction and Q&A, regular 1:1s with a facilitator who follows your practice day to day, and a written curriculum for when you’re stuck. Before the retreat, everyone shares their mental-health history. For hard moments there’s a clear protocol: stop, regulate, slow down, and talk to staff. As for goals, ours is to make it easier to be the person you want to be, with jhana as the training ground. If your goal is different, say so on the call. Some people come mainly to work through emotions, and that’s fine.

    The numbersAdverse effects were logged for 38 of 1,491 participant-retreats (2.5%). 31 have resolved, most within hours or days.

    What helpsBring your goals to the call and ask how a facilitator would work with them.

04

Cost, time and being away

4
  1. A retreat is expensive, and I worry it won’t be worth the money.

    It is expensive. In-person retreats cost about $5,000 and online ones cost less. That’s real money, and if paying would leave you anxious, we’d rather you didn’t come yet. What you get is a week of instruction and 1:1 coaching from a team that has worked with more than 1,400 participants. What you don’t get is a guarantee: 59% reach jhana in a week. The people who don’t still often value the week, but they rate it lower. Cancel 30 or more days before and you get a full refund. If cost is the blocker, ask about scholarships, and try the free options first.

    The numbers59% reached jhana. 64% of those who did called the retreat life-changing, and so did 36% of those who didn’t.

    What helpsDo the free 7-day challenge and the free intro workshop first. If you enjoy them, a retreat is the same practice, much deeper.

  2. I’ll never be able to sit for 8 hours a day.

    Most people think this before they arrive. It is a lot, but it’s probably not what you’re picturing. We suggest 6–8 hours a day, not a fixed 8, and about 3 of those are group sessions with instruction and Q&A. The rest can be sitting, walking or lying down, in whatever posture you can sustain. This isn’t a sit-still-and-endure retreat. We want you to sleep well, and to nap when you’re tired. Because the practice is built on enjoying a pleasant feeling, sits often get longer on their own as the week goes on.

    The numbersThe online work-compatible format asks for about 1.5 hours before and after work on weekdays, plus full days across two weekends.

    What helpsWork up to about 30 minutes of meditation a day before a retreat so that longer sits feel familiar.

  3. I’m worried about disconnecting from the outside world for a week. Something important might happen while I’m gone.

    This is one of the fears people tick most often on retreat, and it’s reasonable: work, family and the news don’t pause for a week. On full-time retreats we strongly encourage unplugging completely, because a week of practice is fragile and checking email pulls you back out. People who kept checking in usually tell us afterwards they wish they hadn’t. Most of what feels urgent can be handled in advance: an out-of-office, a colleague covering, one person who knows how to reach you in an emergency. If a week offline isn’t possible right now, the work-compatible format asks for two weekends instead.

    The numbersIn a sample of 27 completed retreat worksheets, 8 people ticked this fear, the most of any we counted.

    What helpsPick one person who can reach you in a real emergency, tell them how, and let everything else wait.

  4. I’m looking at this because friends said their retreat was awesome. I’m not 100% sure I want to go myself.

    That’s how a lot of people find us. Around half the people we speak with on calls came to us through a friend or partner. A friend’s enthusiasm is a good reason to look, but a weak reason to spend a week. The retreat asks for real engagement, and people do best when the reasons are their own. So it’s worth asking: leaving your friend out of it, what would you want from this? If you find an answer, that’s your reason. If you don’t, it’s fine to wait, or to try something smaller first.

    What helpsDo the free 7-day challenge on your own before deciding. It’ll show you whether the curiosity is yours.

Ticked a few? That’s normal; most people tick several. None of these fears rules you out, and you don’t need to resolve any of them before talking to us. Bring the ones you ticked to your call and we’ll go through them honestly, including when the honest answer is that a retreat isn’t the right next step yet.

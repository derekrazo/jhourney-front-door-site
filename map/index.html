Treat it like a trail map: find roughly where you are, look at what usually keeps people there, and pick one experiment.

Stage 00

Getting ready

You're curious, maybe a little skeptical, and learning what the technique actually asks of you.

What it feels like

You've read about the jhanas and you're intrigued, maybe doubtful. The instructions make sense on paper, but you still have questions, and part of you wonders whether this works for people like you.

From our dataStudents who meditated every day before their retreat reached jhana 71% of the time, against 51% for those who practised twice a week or less (n=70 and n=403, 21 retreats). That's a correlation, but daily practice is a cheap thing to try.

What helps

  • A personal reason this matters to you, beyond 'feeling good': how you want to handle stress, relationships or work
  • Believing you can learn a new skill, even before you can see how
  • Giving the method a fair test, and noticing when that trust slips so you can talk it through
  • Short daily practice before you start anything bigger

Where people get stuck

  • 'This works for other people'

    Feels likeYou suspect you'd need thousands of hours, a traditional teacher, or a different kind of brain.

    TryWrite each doubt as an 'I can't… because…' sentence, then next to it write what evidence would change your mind. A doubt you can name is one you can work with.

  • Reading without digesting

    Feels likeYou've skimmed the instructions and they seem obvious.

    TryExplain the technique out loud to a friend in two minutes. The places you stumble are where your real questions are.

  • Taking the words too literally

    Feels likeYou're confident you've understood every instruction exactly as written.

    TryAsk 'What am I most likely getting wrong?' One student thought 'mantra' meant repeating a phrase nonstop. It's only meant to spark a feeling, and you reuse it when the feeling fades.

This week’s experiment

Write your why

  1. Spend 10 minutes writing what you'd want this practice to change in an ordinary week.
  2. Underline the one line that gives you a small lift when you read it.
  3. List your doubts as 'I can't… because…' sentences and answer each one as honestly as you can.
  4. Reread the underlined line before each sit this week.

Stage 01

Finding a spark

You understand the technique and you're trying things to spark a warm, openhearted feeling.

What it feels like

You know the steps: use a phrase, image, memory or smile to get a warm feeling going, then sink into the feeling itself. Now you're experimenting. Some sits produce a flicker, and plenty produce nothing you can name.

From our dataOn Day 1 of retreat, facilitators rated 23% of students here (67 of 292). By Day 4 it was 2% (7 of 338). Facilitator ratings, 13 retreats, Oct 2024 to Jul 2025.

What helps

  • Trying the common kinds of 'scaffolding': kind wishes ('may you be happy'), a visualization, a warm memory, a smile
  • Giving each one at least 15 minutes before you judge it
  • Two to four hours of total play, in sits of 30 minutes or more
  • Lowering the bar: a faint warmth counts

Where people get stuck

  • Waiting for fireworks

    Feels likeYou're waiting for something dramatic and dismiss the faint warmth that's already there.

    TryCount anything slightly openhearted, even 'not unpleasant'. Rate it out of 10 and treat a 2 as something to build on.

  • Bouncing around

    Feels likeYou switch phrase or image every few minutes because none of them seem to work.

    TryStay with one for 15 minutes before deciding. Try one from each kind, then commit to whichever worked best.

  • Gripping too hard

    Feels likeAs soon as something warm appears you clamp onto it, and it vanishes.

    TryCut your effort in half, then in half again. Think 'open up to it', not 'drill into it'.

  • Looking for a body buzz

    Feels likeYou're hunting for tingles or buzzing with no emotional tone to them.

    TryThis is a feeling meditation. Look for the emotional quality first (gratitude, warmth, friendliness), then notice where it shows up in your body.

This week’s experiment

Scaffolding sampler

  1. Pick three kinds of scaffolding: a phrase of kind wishes, a warm memory, and a gentle smile.
  2. Give each one a 15-minute block within a single 45-minute sit.
  3. After each block, note roughly how often a warm feeling showed up. Even 20% of the time is enough.
  4. Keep the winner for tomorrow, and resist switching.

Stage 02

Keeping the spark

Something reliably sparks a warm feeling, at least some of the time.

What it feels like

Your phrase or image works, sometimes. Then comes a sit where it 'dries up' and you wonder if you broke it. You're starting to notice that how you hold the feeling matters as much as what you're holding.

From our dataThe most common facilitator rating on Day 1: 42% of students (123 of 292), across 13 retreats.

What helps

  • Keeping the same scaffolding and changing how you relate to it: more curiosity, more enjoyment, more relaxation
  • Treating a dry spell as a clue, not a verdict
  • A 'giving away' move: offer the feeling outward to someone and see what comes back
  • A playful attitude, and enough time on the cushion

Where people get stuck

  • Reading 'drying up' as failure

    Feels likeThe phrase that worked yesterday feels flat today, so you go looking for a new one.

    TryKeep it. Ask what changed in how you're relating to it: are you checking, pushing, expecting? The novelty wore off, and the lesson is in what replaced it.

  • Pressing the button harder

    Feels likeYou repeat the phrase harder and faster, hoping joy will show up if you work for it.

    TryTreat it as learning, not weightlifting. Say the phrase once, then rest and look for any tension you're adding before you say it again.

  • Mistaking spacing out for bliss

    Feels likeSits feel calm and pleasant but foggy, and time passes without anything building.

    TryCheck for clarity: could you describe the feeling's texture right now? If not, sit a little more upright and get curious about the details.

  • Grinding through it

    Feels likeMeditation feels like a chore you're getting through.

    TryAsk what would make the next five minutes fun, and do that. One teacher's only two rules for retreat: smile and have fun.

This week’s experiment

Same phrase, new relationship

  1. Use yesterday's scaffolding, unchanged.
  2. Each time it dims, pause and ask: how am I holding this differently from when it worked?
  3. Try one change at a time: relax 5% more, get more curious, or savour it like slipping into a hot tub.
  4. Try giving the feeling away: wish it outward to someone, then notice what happens in you.
  5. Write down which change helped. That's your data for tomorrow.

Stage 03

Settling in

The warm feeling shows up most sits, and you're starting to enjoy yourself.

What it feels like

You can get the feeling going most of the time now. Sometimes you look up and 40 minutes have passed because you were enjoying yourself. Distractions still pull you out, and boredom still shows up.

From our dataThe most common facilitator rating on Day 2: 38% of students (143 of 375), across 13 retreats.

What helps

  • Pockets of genuine fun, however short
  • Welcoming distractions so that each one leaves you a little more relaxed
  • Sits past 45 minutes, which is when many people report settling in
  • Letting the goal sit in the background, like a musician lost in the music

Where people get stuck

  • Sweeping distractions under the rug

    Feels likeYou yank your attention back each time it wanders, and you're no more relaxed at minute 40 than at minute 5.

    TryWhen you notice you've wandered, relax whatever tension came with it, then return gently. Some students greet each distraction with 'come here, old friend'.

  • Running from boredom

    Feels likeYou get restless and end the sit, or reach for something new to do in it.

    TryTreat restlessness as tension you haven't welcomed yet. Stay two more minutes and get curious about what boredom feels like in your body.

  • Stopping when the timer goes off

    Feels likeYou're still doing 30-minute sits because that's what the timer says.

    TryBlock 90 minutes. At 30, 45 and 60 minutes ask: do I want another 5 to 10? What would make that fun? Stop when the honest answer is no.

  • Narrating the sit

    Feels likePart of you is commentating on and evaluating the sit while it happens.

    TryGive the commentator the sit off. Swap a sharp, watchful mind for a soft, tender awareness, and do your reviewing afterwards.

This week’s experiment

The open-ended sit

  1. Block 90 minutes with no timer.
  2. Spend the first five minutes noticing each sound or thought and letting it go as gently as you can.
  3. Bring in your scaffolding and let the warm feeling start.
  4. At 30, 45 and 60 minutes, ask whether you want 5 to 10 more and what would make them fun.
  5. Afterwards, write one thing that made the sit more enjoyable.
← Keeping the spark Finding flow →

Stage 04

Finding flow

Meditation feels like slipping into a hot tub, and you're losing track of time.

What it feels like

You want to sit, and hour-long sits happen on their own. Distractions have started to feed the sit instead of breaking it. You can sense something bigger nearby, which makes it tempting to reach for it.

From our dataThe most common stage from Day 3 to Day 5, and the last recorded stage for 30% of students (135 of 449). Facilitator ratings, 13 retreats.

What helps

  • Letting go of the outcome while keeping a direction
  • A step up in how fully you let yourself relax, savour and get curious
  • Sits of 60 to 90 minutes, a few times a day
  • Carrying enjoyment and relaxation between sits, so each one starts warmer
  • Switching to forgiveness or self-compassion when something heavy comes up

Where people get stuck

  • Reaching for jhana

    Feels likeYou can feel it's close, so you push toward it, and it recedes.

    TryTo get into jhana you have to let go of wanting to. Set the intention at the start of the sit, then forget it, like a child absorbed in building a sandcastle.

  • Bracing when it builds

    Feels likeThe feeling grows strong and something in you tightens or pulls back.

    TryLean into the intensity, then lean back into something that feels safe, and repeat. Each round, let it be a little bigger than last time.

  • Checking whether this is it

    Feels likeAs soon as something new happens, you start analysing it, and the state wobbles.

    TryMake a deal with yourself: no labelling until after the sit. Relax into the body instead of observing the sensations.

  • Drifting into dullness

    Feels likeLong sits slowly turn foggy and sleepy instead of bright and absorbed.

    TryAdd brightness: more curiosity, a slight smile, a more upright posture. Let the feeling be interesting, not just restful.

This week’s experiment

Five percent more

  1. Get the feeling going as usual.
  2. Every few minutes ask: can I enjoy this 5% more? Relax 5% deeper? Get 5% more curious?
  3. When it starts to build, let it be bigger than you expected instead of steering it.
  4. If you tighten, meet the tightening with kindness. If you can't love the feeling, love the resistance to it.

Stage 05 Jhana

First jhana

A distinct shift: you're pulled into joy and ease that keeps itself going.

What it feels like

Something tips over. The good feeling stops needing you to hold it up, and you can notice it or even get distracted without losing it. Afterwards there's an afterglow and, for a while, less craving for other things.

From our dataAmong students who reached jhana, the median first jhana came on Day 3, and 43% got there on Day 4 or later (n=619).

What helps

  • Noticing what you did just before it happened
  • Going back to the basics that got you here, instead of chasing a repeat
  • Self-compassion or forgiveness for anything that resurfaces
  • Patience: a first jhana is often followed by a few quiet sits

Where people get stuck

  • Chasing the memory

    Feels likeEvery sit becomes an attempt to recreate last time, and nothing happens.

    TryTreat last time as proof it's possible, not as a target. Start from your scaffolding as if it were your first day and let the sit go where it goes.

  • Getting stuck on 'was that it?'

    Feels likeYou spend sits wondering whether it counted, or feel let down that it was only pleasant.

    TryDrop the verdict. People who stop worrying about whether it was jhana often sink much deeper. Spend your attention in the state, not in judging it.

  • A quiet block comes back

    Feels likeSomething you thought was settled (worry, sadness, self-judgement) is back and keeping things shallow.

    TrySwitch to forgiveness or self-compassion for part of the sit. You don't need to process everything, only what's stopping you from sinking in.

This week’s experiment

Stay, don't chase

  1. When a good state arises, don't try to deepen it or move on. Just stay.
  2. Notice what keeps it going: relaxing, enjoying, getting curious, or giving up control.
  3. After the sit, write down the last thing you did before it started.
  4. Next sit, try that move once, then let go of the result.

Stage 06 Jhana

Sometimes jhana

You get into jhana once or more a day, but can't count on it in any given sit.

What it feels like

You know the territory now. Some sits drop in within an hour and others never do, and you can't always tell why. You're starting to recognize the move that works for you.

From our dataThe last recorded stage for 13% of students (60 of 449). Facilitator ratings, 13 retreats.

What helps

  • A repeatable move: letting go of control, letting curiosity overwhelm you, fully releasing tension, stacking enjoyment, or self-compassion all the way down
  • Welcoming nearly every distraction
  • Lots of 60 to 90 minute sits
  • Basic skill with forgiveness for blocks that show up

Where people get stuck

  • Too few long sits

    Feels likeYour sits are getting shorter and access feels hit-or-miss.

    TryGoing from 'sometimes' to 'reliably' is mostly repetitions. Prioritise 60 to 90 minute sits for a few days.

  • Starting from zero every sit

    Feels likeEach sit starts cold because the time in between is rushed or stressful.

    TryCarry the feeling between sits: walk slowly, eat with enjoyment, leave your phone off. Let the next sit begin where the last one ended.

  • Missing the new tension

    Feels likeThe move that worked yesterday doesn't work today.

    TryLook for a new kind of tension. Yesterday it might have been about approval, today about the future. Spotting it is the hard part; once you see it, welcoming it usually comes easily.

This week’s experiment

Find your move

  1. After every sit, write two lines: what you did just before any shift, and roughly how long it took.
  2. After three or four sits, look for the pattern.
  3. Name your move in a few words, such as 'let it overwhelm me' or 'soften the jaw and savour'.
  4. Start the next sit with that move, then let it go and see what happens.

Stage 07 Jhana

Jhana on demand

You can enter jhana in almost any sit on retreat, and in under 90 minutes at home.

What it feels like

Getting in has become a skill rather than an event. You know your move and roughly how long it takes. The open question now is how deep this goes.

From our data51% of students who reached jhana left their retreat with on-demand access (329 of 647 who answered the exit survey).

What helps

  • A reliable move, and a feel for how long it takes
  • Noticing where in the body the feeling starts and grows
  • Spending plenty of time in jhana when it comes
  • Carrying curiosity, relaxation and enjoyment through the day

Where people get stuck

  • Thinking you've 'got it'

    Feels likeYou've reached the goal, so practice starts to feel optional.

    TryThe jhanas keep getting richer, and practice time compounds here. Set a curiosity goal for your next sits, not a performance goal.

  • Grabbing at the next jhana

    Feels likeYou push from one jhana to the next and partly succeed, but each one stays shallow.

    TryUse the move that got you in: spot what's tense and soften it. Going deeper is more subtraction than effort.

  • Expecting jhana to solve everything

    Feels likeYou're a little let down that reliable jhana didn't change the rest of your life on its own.

    TryThe skill underneath the states, spotting and releasing tension, is the part that carries into ordinary life. Notice one place it showed up today.

This week’s experiment

Subtract to go deeper

  1. Enter jhana as usual and rest there for a while.
  2. Notice what in the experience feels slightly coarse, busy or tiring.
  3. Soften that one thing, the way you released tension to get in.
  4. Wait. Let the shift happen on its own rather than pushing for it.

Stage 08 Jhana

Moving through the jhanas

You move up and down the first four jhanas, and see the same move repeating at finer levels.

What it feels like

The release that got you into the first jhana turns out to be the same release that takes you to the second, then the third, each time more subtly. Moving between them is starting to feel fluid. From here people choose a direction: the later jhanas, faster access, or using jhana practice for personal change.

From our dataThe last recorded stage for 3% of students (12 of 449). Facilitator ratings, 13 retreats.

What helps

  • Plenty of time in jhana
  • Letting your nervous system move on by itself instead of steering
  • A generous, kind attitude toward every part of the sit
  • Seeing each step as letting go, not adding

Where people get stuck

  • Steering instead of letting it settle

    Feels likeYou keep trying to move to the next jhana on schedule.

    TryStay put and let yourself soak. Notice the moment your system moves on by itself.

  • Treating the states as the point

    Feels likeYou measure progress only by which jhana you reached.

    TryCheck what has changed off the cushion: how quickly you recover from stress, how you meet a hard conversation. Steadiness there is what makes the states worth it.

This week’s experiment

Let it soak

  1. Enter a jhana and decide you won't try to go anywhere else.
  2. Rest there, kindly, with nothing to do.
  3. If a shift comes, let it come without helping it along.
  4. Afterwards, note whether it moved on by itself and how long that took.
← Jhana on demand

It isn't a test or a scorecard. People don't move in a straight line. Some skip stages, some loop back, and a first jhana sometimes shows up early and then disappears for a few days. All of that is normal. If you catch yourself reading this competitively, that's useful to notice too. See if you can read it with curiosity instead.

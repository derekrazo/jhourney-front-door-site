For retreat alumni · your first 30 days

Keeping it

What to do with what you found, from the last day of retreat to Day 30.

The retreat gave you the skills. What happens next is where they get used: in your actual life, with its inbox, its family dinners and its old triggers. Some of what you feel right now will fade, and that’s expected. This page is for keeping the part that matters.

01

The first week

Land softly

For a few days your senses may feel extra vivid and your emotions closer to the surface. Colours look brighter. A song in a shop hits harder than it should. This is normal and temporary, and every contemplative tradition describes it.

The retreat turned your sensitivity up on purpose; it’s what made the week work. Now you’re carrying that sensitivity back into an environment built for someone less sensitive. Think of re-entry as recalibration. It can feel like playing your life on hard mode for a bit. That’s not a sign something went wrong. It’s the next level of the same game.

One rule of thumb from our facilitators: one quiet buffer day for every week you were on retreat.

  • Plan the buffer day before you leave. Talk relatively little, skip work email and messages, and don’t doom-scroll. Walk somewhere green, do a hobby you love, see people who are easy to be around.
  • Sit once a day, at roughly the same time, even if it’s short. Ten good minutes beat forty grim ones. The first two or three weeks are when a home practice is easiest to set, because the retreat is still close.
  • Write down what you found while it’s fresh. Which moves worked for you? What did the good sits feel like right before they opened up? You’ll want these notes in Week 3.
  • Reread your letter to yourself from the retreat, especially the part about obstacles. Those are usually the first things waiting for you at home.
  • Keep your litmus tests. Wonder, relaxation and enjoyment. If a sit has those, it’s working, whatever else happens.

02

Weeks two to four

The fade is part of it

The afterglow will fade. In our 2024 alumni survey it tended to wear off within a few days to a few weeks, as ordinary life crept back in. One alum put it like this: “In that first week, I maintained the intensity. By the second week, that intensity had started to drop off, the clarity of each jhana started to fade.”

Access can fade too. By our facilitators’ judgement, about half of the people who reach jhana on retreat (51%, 329 of 647) leave able to get back in reliably. Even then it comes and goes. Without a week of protected time and a group practising alongside you, it’s simply harder.

So here’s the reframe. The afterglow isn’t the benefit. It’s a temporary boost, like a runner’s high. The benefits people describe months later are quieter: kinder to themselves, calmer, more aware of small pleasures, knowing what “good” feels like from the inside. Those don’t depend on getting back into jhana tomorrow.

Treat the fade like a game. You learned to level up once, in a week. Now you’re learning to do it in your real conditions, which is harder and worth more.

  • Afterglow: vivid senses, unusual ease, buzzy calm. Temporary by nature.
  • Benefit: what still shows up on a bad day. A little more space before you react. A kinder inner voice after a mistake.
  • If you’re only tracking the first, you’ll think you’re losing. Track the second.

03

When it stops working

If you suddenly can’t get back in

This is the most useful signal you’ll get. One line from the retreat booklet is worth taping to your wall: if you suddenly can’t enter jhana, it’s a clear sign it’s time to go looking for the emotion you’ve started avoiding.

It’s rarely that you’ve lost the skill. More often something in your life is asking for attention, and the open-hearted feeling can’t get past it. Try these in order and treat each one as an experiment. There’s no failure, just data.

  • Stop chasing. Notice how hard you’re trying, then cut it in half. Then in half again. Grasping at the old state is the most common way to keep it away.
  • Ask what you’ve been avoiding. Before your next sit, take two minutes: what feeling have I been too busy for this week? Irritation at work, worry about someone, grief, a conversation you keep postponing.
  • Turn towards it with warmth. Let the feeling be there, and bring in a sense of safety and self-compassion alongside it. You’re not trying to make it go away. You’re keeping it company until it moves.
  • Go back a step. Spend whole sits on the pre-jhana ground: the open-hearted feeling, forgiveness, or simply sinking into feeling safe in your body. Those are productive sits in their own right.
  • Shorten, don’t skip. On high-stress weeks, five or ten minutes a day keeps the thread. Save the long sits for days when you have room.
  • Get help if it’s heavy. If difficult feelings are coming up faster than you can be with them, talk to someone: a friend, a professional, or us at retreats@jhourney.io. Bring it to your integration call too.

04

If you didn’t reach jhana

The same game, without the shortcut

If jhana didn’t happen for you this week, you’re in good company: about four in ten people finish a retreat without it (41%, by our facilitators’ count across 1,436 participants). Meanwhile, the work that changes your emotional defaults doesn’t require jhana. It’s faster with it, not impossible without it.

Some of the most productive sits look like this: gently asking what emotion you’ve been avoiding, practising forgiveness, or deepening into a felt sense of safety or self-compassion. Those are the same moves that open the door to jhana, so you’re practising both at once.

One alum, a while after their retreat: “Even though I haven’t experienced a true Jhana state since the retreat, I don’t feel deficient in any way… Just the practice and process continues to add immense value to my everyday life.”

  • Keep the techniques you used all week. They work off the cushion too.
  • Watch for “jhana-like” moments in ordinary life: a long exhale in the sun, music that lands. Alumni who keep practising describe noticing more of these.
  • Tell your integration call where you got to. It’s the most useful thing we can know.

05

The people around you

Others weren’t on retreat with you

Your new habits will change how you respond to people. They won’t be expecting it. You might be more patient with someone who used to wind you up, or finally hold a boundary you’ve been avoiding, kindly. Either can confuse people at first. Expect it to take several repetitions before they adjust.

You’ll also want to talk about the week. In our survey, alumni processed it with friends and loved ones about as often as with our staff. That’s good: the people at home matter for integration. Just know that describing jhana to someone who hasn’t felt it rarely lands. Describe what changed for you instead.

  • Before: your partner says something critical and your chest tightens. After: the same comment lands. You notice the tightness, and there’s space around it. That space is the thing to protect.
  • Lead with what you noticed (“I’ve been calmer in meetings”), not with the states.
  • Find one meditation buddy, ideally from your cohort. Sitting together and comparing notes keeps momentum and builds unusually good friendships.

Your rhythm

Pick a rhythm

We’ve seen three ways people build a practice that lasts. None is better. Pick the one that fits your life, or mix two. Whichever you choose, quality beats quantity: the people who progress on relatively little practice are the ones who practise in small moments all day and stay curious about it.

01

Daily practice

Best if you like routine and can protect a regular slot, morning or evening.

  • Find the shortest daily sit that still moves you forward. For some people that’s 20 minutes; for others an hour.
  • Same time, same place. Keep a cushion or bench by your bed so starting takes no effort.
  • Keep a short log and do a weekly check-in with yourself: what worked, what’s next to try.

Watch forTurning it into a chore. If “I should sit” replaces “I want to see what happens”, shorten it and make it fun again.

02

Periodic retreats

Best if daily sitting hasn’t stuck before, or you go deepest with long blocks of time.

  • Do two to four retreats a year. Skip daily practice, or keep a light one alongside.
  • Book the next one while this one is fresh, so it’s on the calendar before life fills it.
  • Use the gaps for micro-practices so the thread doesn’t snap completely.

Watch forLong gaps where nothing happens. If you go months without sitting, the first days of the next retreat go on re-warming.

03

Monthly mini-retreats

Best if weekdays are packed but you can clear a whole day now and then.

  • Take one or two days a month for 6–8 hours of practice. Treat them like a real retreat day: phone away, simple food, walking practice.
  • Put them in the calendar for the next three months now.
  • Start the day with a quiet morning, the way the retreat did.

Watch forLetting them slip. A mini-retreat that isn’t in the calendar tends not to happen.

Small things, every day

  1. 01

    Two-minute alarms

    Set two or three alarms a day for a 2–5 minute mini-sit. Close your eyes, find the open-hearted feeling, see how close it is today.

  2. 02

    Use the gaps

    Between meetings, before you open your laptop, after you park the car: pause, notice how you feel, set an intention for the next thing.

  3. 03

    “What’s your state?”

    Put a few sticky notes around the house with that question. Check the actual feeling, not the assumed one. Sometimes “hungry” turns out to be irritated.

  4. 04

    Same soundtrack

    Do focused work to the same music you meditate to. The two start to borrow from each other.

  5. 05

    Lower the bar to start

    Cushion next to the bed. A meditation book where you’ll see it. The less effort it takes to begin, the more often you will.

  6. 06

    Ride the good sits

    If a sit is unusually rich, and you can, clear your schedule and let it go long. Be opportunistic about what’s working.

  7. 07

    Catch the avoidance

    When you notice yourself doom-scrolling or procrastinating, ask what feeling it’s keeping away. Naming it is a micro-practice.

Fragile, resilient, antifragile

One of our facilitators kept a mostly daily hour of practice for five years, after three years that had ended in burnout. Looking back, his practice went through three phases. Most people start in the first one. That’s not a warning; it’s the map.

  1. Fragile

    You lose the habit now and then, and your wellbeing dips when you do. This is where nearly everyone is in the first month. The job here is just to keep coming back, without making the lapses mean anything.

  2. Resilient

    Hard things still come up, at work or at home, but they get processed and your wellbeing stays fairly high through them. The practice is now something you use, not something you keep up.

  3. Antifragile

    Deeper material surfaces, and working through it leaves you better off than before. In his words, his personality got lighter over time. That took years, not weeks, so don’t grade yourself against it in Week 2.

You’re not doing this alone

You’re not doing this alone

The alumni community is where people from every cohort compare notes after the retreat. It’s the easiest place to find a meditation buddy, ask “is this normal?”, and see that everyone’s practice fades and comes back. Start here, then say hello.

  • Every week, a “keep” prompt. One small question or experiment. Reply with what happened, in a line or two.
  • Find a buddy. Post your time zone and the rhythm you’re trying. Sitting together, even on video, keeps both of you going.
  • Share your Day 7 and Day 30 answers if you’re comfortable. Other people’s answers are often the most useful thing on the page.
  • Come to community sits. Practising with other alumni is the closest thing to the retreat container at home.

Cut the effort in half. Then do it again.

Why the people who try hardest at meditation often get stuck longest, and a 10-minute experiment to see it for yourself.

Stephen Zerfas · adapted from The Pragmatic Guide to the Jhanas

“Take whatever effort you're planning to apply to this challenge, and cut it in half. Then, do so again.”

Most skills punish insufficient effort. We learn early that hard work is a virtue, and in most domains it pays.

Meditation looks like it should follow the same logic. Sit longer. Focus harder. Be more disciplined. Bring the mind back every time it wanders.

In our experience teaching the jhanas, a set of meditative states that run from intense joy to deep peace, this is the single most common way people get stuck. Over-efforting is almost certainly the most common antipattern we see in novices, and it tends to hide even longer in experienced meditators.

One teacher we admire says, “There are only two rules on this retreat: smile and have fun.” They're two excellent rules. They're also, unfortunately, hard for many of our students to follow.

Mental tension

To see why, you need one idea we lean on more than any other: mental tension.

Mental tension is a faster-than-thought, often subtle sense of contraction or tightness that, if you pay attention, is present to varying degrees in all kinds of experience, especially difficult ones. It starts vague and abstract and gets clearer with practice.

You already know how to release it. You just don't have a name for it. It's the move you make when you open yourself up to the possibility of changing your mind. Or the moment when you apologize to a loved one and mean it. Or the shift that lets you be filled with awe on a hike, instead of ruminating on the day's events.

Some of our favorite pointers:

  • In this moment, how much of your experience are you bracing vs. embracing?
  • Suffering = pain × resistance.
  • Most people have no problem sorting emotions into two categories: “open” and “closed.” Closed emotions usually carry more mental tension.

The equation is the one to sit with. You often can't do much about the pain. Resistance is the multiplier you have some say over. Imagine maxing out a lift while ruminating on the inadequacy of your strength, versus celebrating the miracle of your body. Same weight. Very different experience.

Goals create tension

Here's where effort comes in. Romeo Stevens uses a metaphor I like: goals work like hydroelectric dams. Setting one builds up tension in the nervous system, and that tension powers action until the goal is met and the dam releases.

In daily life that's mostly fine. In meditation it's a trap. Every “I need to relax,” every “am I making progress?”, every “is this it?” builds a new dam while you're trying to take down the old ones.

This is why many teachers say “there is no goal.” We disagree. There's always a goal, or you wouldn't sit down. But the statement is useful, because most of us pursue goals in a way that works against releasing tension.

Goals without tension

Anyone who's slipped into flow or play knows you can lose all sense of self-monitoring without losing direction. Tension isn't the only engine. It's just the one most of us default to.

So we ask students to set one goal above all the others: learn to spot and then release mental tension of all kinds, including the tension that comes from goals. Everything else stacks under it, from the experiment you want to run in this sit to the skill you want to build this month. Over time you get better at feeling which goals are pulling at you, and at choosing which ones to keep.

The over-efforting trap

Our external environments select for internal thought habits. If you worked hard to get into college and worked hard to get a job you want, consider that the seemingly trite advice “the key is to relax” is not trite but, in fact, something you may not even know how to do.

Unless you think you're in the top 10% of the population in the world in terms of mental habits with respect to relaxing, do not blow past this advice: Take whatever effort you're planning to apply to this challenge, and cut it in half. Then, do so again.

For most students, part of learning the jhanas is discovering ways they've been holding mental tension and applying mental effort without realizing it. So it's not just about relaxing, but learning to relax.

Experienced meditators often have a subtler version: yanking attention back to the breath every time it wanders. One of the most experienced meditators we've had on retreat, who had even been in the jhanas years before, did this for days before he spotted it. He called it “sweeping distractions under the rug.”

Here's the test. If you're not getting progressively more relaxed through your sit, you're almost certainly doing some version of this. Each distraction is an opportunity to let go of a little tension, not a mistake to correct.

Fun is a measurement

That's why “smile and have fun” isn't decoration. Wonder, relaxation, and enjoyment are the readouts. If they aren't arising without effort, that's a signal to change what you're doing, not to push harder.

If you're genuinely not having fun, ask what you might change so that you will. If you're starting to have fun, ask how you know. What in the body tells you that you're enjoying yourself? Can you find where that feeling is strongest?

A 10-minute experiment

Sit somewhere comfortable and set a gentle timer for ten minutes. Pick something simple to rest on, like the breath or a pleasant memory.

  1. Minutes 0–3: your usual effort. Meditate the way you normally would. Don't change anything. Just notice how hard you're gripping: in the forehead, the jaw, the attention itself.
  2. Minutes 3–6: half the effort. Same object, half the grip. When the mind wanders, don't drag it back. Drop the distraction as quickly, as gently, and as relaxingly as you can, soften something in the body, then return.
  3. Minutes 6–9: half again. Ask, lightly: how much of my experience am I bracing vs. embracing right now? You don't need to answer in words. Let whatever you notice soften a little more.
  4. The last minute: smile. Even a small one. Ask, “What about this is pleasant?” and let your attention settle on the answer.

What to look for

Afterwards, jot down three things: what went well, what didn't, and what you'd try next. If you got more relaxed as the ten minutes went on, that's the signal you're on the right track. If you didn't, that's useful too. It usually means some effort is hiding somewhere you haven't looked yet. There's no failure, just data.

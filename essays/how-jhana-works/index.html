How jhana works, for engineers

Four working models of a poorly understood mechanism, and what each one changes about how you practice.

Stephen Zerfas · adapted from The Pragmatic Guide to the Jhanas

“We see ourselves as a system – complex and probabilistic rather than deterministic, but nonetheless understandable and responsive to intervention.”

Here's an honest starting point: the causal mechanisms of jhana are still poorly understood. By us, and by everyone else.

If you build things for a living, that might sound like a reason to wait. I'd argue the opposite. Engineers use models they know are incomplete all the time. A model earns its place by predicting something and suggesting what to try next. We use ours the same way: because we find them useful, not because we're claiming they're true.

Quick context if you're new here. The jhanas are a set of meditative states that run from intense joy to deep peace. We teach them on week-long retreats. Below is the frame we start from, then three models we use with students, then what each one changes about practice.

The frame: see yourself as a system

We see ourselves as a system – complex and probabilistic rather than deterministic, but nonetheless understandable and responsive to intervention.

The system has conscious and subconscious parts. We have limited control over a few conscious inputs:

  • The focus and direction of our attention
  • Our relationship to what we notice in our attention
  • (Sometimes) our intentions

How we manage those inputs moment by moment gradually shapes the subconscious, where our emotional reactions and automatic behaviors live. Two properties matter: we have minimal control over the present moment, and skillful use of that limited control can shape our subconscious and personality over time.

You might think of jhanas as states that emerge from the subconscious on a delay after we wield our attention, our relation to attention, and our intention in specific ways. You don't flip a switch. You set up the conditions and the system responds.

Model 1: a positive feedback loop between attention and emotion

This is our default introduction, and it's easiest to see in reverse. Everyone knows how anxiety captures attention, which produces more anxiety, which captures more attention, until the body joins in: racing heart, sweaty palms, a knot in the throat. That's a panic attack.

Jhana meditators build the same loop with a positive emotion. It's as if the positive emotion quiets the rest of the mind-body system, allowing you to attend to the emotion with more sensitivity and stability, which further quiets the system, which allows you to attend even closer, and so on.

Treat it like any feedback system and the design rules fall out:

  • The signal has to be emotional. Mindlessly repeating a mantra, or attending to purely physical tingles, doesn't feed the loop. The emotion does.
  • Continuity matters. If attention isn't continuous, the flywheel loses momentum.
  • There's an inflection point. Early anxiety is easy to switch off; full panic isn't. Likewise you may need to “kindle the fire” before it takes on a life of its own.
  • Initial conditions matter. A strong starting emotion helps. We've seen new parents learn a lot about jhanas by playing with the joy they feel at the thought of their children.

The most useful debugging question this model gives you: what about this experience is pleasurable? Asking it again and again zeroes in on the most positive part of the feeling and turns the flywheel faster.

Model 2: generous intent as the input

The second model moves the input upstream, from attention to intent. It's closest to how we teach on retreat.

The idea is that feelings like joy, happiness and love are a byproduct of generous intent. We're social creatures. Wishing someone well produces a warm, prosocial emotion. You can then fold that emotion back into the intent (“may they feel this too”), which produces more of it, and so on. Same loop, different driver.

As a rough allocation of attention, we suggest about 70% on the emotion itself, especially its most pleasurable parts in the body, 20% on the intent to share it, and 10% on minimal scaffolding like a phrase or image, which you drop once it's no longer useful.

Model 3: a threshold in a modular mind

Robert Wright, author of Why Buddhism Is True, describes what he calls the Modular Model of the Mind. Rather than a single “I,” we're a collection of modules, each somewhat simplistically concerned with one thing: hunger, approval at work, a life dream. “I” is more like a herd of cats or an under-9 soccer team. As Wright puts it, the “CEO self is an illusion.”

This gives a working definition of mental tension: the faster-than-thought internal violence that occurs when parts of us conflict.

It also explains why the usual instruction to “concentrate” misfires. You don't enter a jhana by effortfully holding attention on an object. Focus and stability come from relaxation. We prefer the word collectedness: gradually collecting the modules into one shared experience. Each “distraction” is really traction, another module you haven't brought in yet. Viewed this way, jhanas are moments of unusual consensus, and each one is another threshold in releasing tension at subtler levels.

The practical consequence is blunt. You'd never coach an under-9 soccer team like a drill sergeant. Our instinct to try harder is often exactly the wrong move, which is why we tell students: whatever effort you're planning to apply, cut it in half. Then cut it in half again.

The analogy: brains build up internal stresses too, and high-energy states like jhana may let them release and resettle. We hold this loosely. But it produced a concrete experiment. One of our instructors started relaxing his expectations about what would happen, letting sensation overwhelm him rather than holding it as an anchor. He found it opened a new chapter of sensory clarity and deepened his jhana access. The model also suggests the afterglow is the “cooling” phase, so what you do right after a sit matters. Drifting off to sleep beats switching on the TV.

Why hold several models at once

Each model has strengths and blind spots, and jhanas are a gift that keeps on giving: you can enter them from different starting places, use them for different purposes, and your experience of them will change over time. The map is not the territory. The models are for generating experiments, not settling debates.

Treating practice this way seems to work. Across 1,436 people with a known outcome on our retreats, 58.7% reached jhana, as judged by staff from their own descriptions. Among those who did, the median first jhana came on Day 3.

A debugging heuristic

If you're stuck and you don't know why, you likely need to increase sensitivity and ask what mental tension is in your experience that you have not yet recognized. And once you've spotted it, the game is to find ways to open up to, relax, or otherwise release it.

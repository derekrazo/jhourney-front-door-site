Mini-guide

Jhourney vs. the alternatives, honestly

About 4 in 10 people we talk to are weighing us against something else: a Vipassana course, a stack of books, an app, a psilocybin retreat, therapy. That's a sensible thing to do. Each one is good at something, and several are free or close to it. Here is our honest read of where each one wins, what it costs in money and time, and where we're different. We've tried to be fair, including about our own weak spots.

01

A 10-day Vipassana course (Goenka tradition)

A residential course in S.N. Goenka's insight-meditation method: breath awareness, then systematically observing body sensations. Teaching is given through recordings of Goenka, with an assistant teacher on site.

CostFree (donation-based; donations accepted only from people who have completed a course).

Time10 days of practice, 12 calendar days including arrival and departure. About 10 hours of meditation a day.

Best forPeople who want a deep, disciplined immersion in one well-tested insight method and can take 12 days off. It also suits anyone for whom cost is the deciding factor.

Where it wins

  • Free. Food and lodging are covered by donations from past students, and first-timers aren't asked to pay.
  • Centers all over the world, a very consistent format, and decades of track record.
  • Real intensity: about 10 hours of meditation a day in full silence builds a lot of steadiness.
  • A clear lineage and a large community you can keep sitting with afterwards.

Trade-offs

  • 12 calendar days, with a 4 a.m. wake-up, and you commit to staying for the whole course.
  • One method, followed as taught. Changing technique isn't part of the format.
  • Less individual feedback: a midday slot for questions to the teacher and a short evening Q&A.
  • Not aimed at the jhanas or at pleasant states. Many people describe it as hard going.

How Jhourney differsVipassana aims at insight through equanimity, and it's free, which we admire. Jhourney is narrower: we teach the jhanas, starting with relaxation, warmth and curiosity rather than body scanning. Because we don't assume one method fits everyone, you get far more 1:1 time and you're encouraged to change technique. Our founders came to the jhanas after Goenka courses, and plenty of our students do both.

02

Teaching yourself from books and recorded teachers

Learning from books and recorded talks such as Leigh Brasington's Right Concentration, The Mind Illuminated, Path to Nibbana or Rob Burbea's Practising the Jhānas, practicing at home on your own schedule.

Cost$0 to about $30. Recorded talks are free; books are about $15-30.

TimeOpen-ended: usually a daily practice over months, sometimes with occasional retreats.

Best forSelf-motivated people with a steady daily practice who are happy to go slowly, and anyone who wants to understand the traditions in depth.

Where it wins

  • Nearly free. Burbea's talks are free to stream and download, and a book costs about as much as lunch.
  • Depth and nuance. These are some of the best teachers alive or recently alive. We recommend them ourselves, and our own curriculum draws on Brasington, TWIM and Burbea.
  • Fits around any life, with no travel and no time off.
  • You can go at your own pace for years.

Trade-offs

  • No feedback. It's hard to tell whether you're over-efforting or what to change, and that's the most common way people stall.
  • Slow for most people. Without retreat conditions, progress usually takes months or years.
  • Different books define jhana differently, which can be confusing if you're new.

How Jhourney differsWe teach from the same sources. What you're paying for is the container: a week of retreat conditions plus someone who spots where you're stuck and suggests the next experiment. If you're already making steady progress alone, keep going, and use our free resources to fill gaps.

03

Meditation apps (Headspace, Calm, Waking Up)

Guided meditations and courses on your phone, usually 5-30 minutes a day.

CostAbout $70-150 a year (Headspace and Calm about $69.99 a year; Waking Up $149.99 a year in the US App Store).

Time5-30 minutes a day, open-ended.

Best forBuilding a daily habit, reducing everyday stress, getting better sleep, and exploring meditation at very low cost and commitment.

Where it wins

  • Cheap and always available.
  • Excellent for getting started and for consistency: a few minutes a day, anywhere.
  • Some, like Waking Up, include serious teachers and ideas, not just relaxation.
  • No time off, no travel, no risk to speak of.

Trade-offs

  • No feedback on what's actually happening in your practice.
  • Few people stick with them. In the study we often cite, most users completed only a handful of sessions and under 5% were still using an app after 30 days.
  • Sessions are generally too short and too light for jhana practice.

How Jhourney differsApps are a good on-ramp, and many of our students started with one. A retreat is a different tool: a concentrated week aimed at one specific, learnable skill, with a person checking your work. Some alumni keep using an app afterwards for short daily sits.

04

A psilocybin retreat or legal session

A guided psilocybin experience, either at a multi-day retreat (for example in the Netherlands, where psilocybin truffles are legal) or at a licensed service center in Oregon. Most include screening, preparation and integration.

CostRetreats: about $1,400-6,000 (Netherlands, 3-5 days). Oregon licensed sessions: up to about $3,000 including preparation and integration.

Time1 day to about 5 days, plus preparation and integration sessions.

Best forAdults with no contraindications who want a single, powerful experience that opens their view of what's possible, in a legal, supervised setting.

Where it wins

  • Very reliable: almost everyone who takes an adequate dose has a strong altered experience. No skill is required.
  • Fast: one day or a long weekend.
  • For many people it's the first proof that states like this exist at all. A lot of our students first touched this territory with psychedelics.

Trade-offs

  • It happens to you, not by you. You can't call the state up again on a Tuesday morning.
  • Legal only in some places. Medical and psychiatric contraindications apply, and a difficult experience is possible.
  • The comedown is real, and repeating it costs the same each time.

How Jhourney differsSome people describe the deeper jhanas as similar to parts of a psychedelic experience, but the jhanas are a skill you build and can return to without a substance. Jhana practice is slower and less certain than a dose, but what you learn stays with you. Please don't combine the two: we ask that people take no psychedelics during a retreat.

05

Therapy

Ongoing work with a licensed therapist on specific problems, history or patterns, usually weekly.

CostTypically about $65-250 a session out of pocket in the US, less with insurance. Weekly for a year comes to several thousand dollars.

TimeAbout an hour a week, usually for months.

Best forAnyone dealing with trauma, depression, anxiety, a crisis, or a pattern they want a trained professional to help them understand and change. Also anyone currently unstable.

Where it wins

  • Designed for mental-health problems, which a meditation retreat is not.
  • A trained, accountable professional who gets to know you over months or years.
  • Can be covered by insurance.
  • Safe for people in acute distress, who should not start with an intensive retreat.

Trade-offs

  • Mostly talk-based, and it often doesn't give you a practice for changing your mood from the inside.
  • Adds up over time: weekly sessions for months.
  • Quality and fit vary a lot between therapists.

How Jhourney differsThese aren't competitors. A retreat is training, not treatment, and we screen out people in acute distress. Alumni (and our own facilitators) often say the practice and therapy worked well together: therapy helps you understand a pattern, and jhana practice gives you a felt sense of safety to meet it with. If you're struggling right now, start with therapy.

06

A traditionally taught jhana retreat

A silent retreat at a dharma center taught by a teacher in a Buddhist lineage, usually 7-10+ days. Jhana is taught as part of a wider path that includes ethics and insight practice.

CostRoughly $300-800 for 10-day online versions and about $1,000-2,700 for 10-night residential ones (sliding scale), plus a voluntary donation to the teacher.

TimeUsually 7-10+ days, silent. Often requires prior retreats.

Best forExperienced meditators who want jhana within its Buddhist context and lineage, often with a deeper or stricter standard of absorption.

Where it wins

  • Teachers with decades of practice and a lineage behind them.
  • Jhana taught in context, connected to insight, ethics and the rest of the path.
  • Often a lower fee, with teacher pay offered by donation (dana) and sliding scales.
  • Some traditions teach deeper absorption than we aim for.

Trade-offs

  • Often require prior retreat experience. One center we checked asks for two previous 7-day silent retreats.
  • Usually one teacher's method and less 1:1 time.
  • Not designed for beginners or for people who want to stay secular.

How Jhourney differsWe owe a lot to these teachers, and we'll happily refer you to one. Our version is secular, open to complete beginners, and aims at the more accessible jhanas, the ones described in the suttas, rather than the deepest absorption. It also has far more individual coaching. If lineage and the full Buddhist path matter to you, a traditional retreat is probably the better fit.

Which is right for you

If you want…

  1. I'm curious but not ready to spend anything

    →

    Our free intro-to-jhana workshop, the free 7-day challenge, or a book like Right Concentration

    You'll find out whether this way of practicing appeals to you before you spend money or time off.

  2. I want more joy day to day, and I can't take a week off

    →

    Jhourney online, work-compatible

    Two retreat weekends with your normal workweek in between. It costs the same as the online full-time retreat, and you only need to take two Fridays off.

  3. I want to try a full day of practice before committing to a week

    →

    A Jhourney online one-day retreat

    A condensed taste of the curriculum for a few hundred dollars. Reps can usually credit it toward a full retreat later.

  4. I want to learn the jhanas as fast and reliably as I can

    →

    A Jhourney retreat, full-time online or in person

    Retreat conditions plus frequent 1:1 feedback. 58.7% reached jhana in the week, with online and in-person results close (57.5% vs 60.1%).

  5. I want a deep insight retreat and money is tight

    →

    A 10-day Goenka Vipassana course

    It's free, it's everywhere, and it's a serious training in steadiness. It isn't a jhana retreat, and it's rigorous.

  6. I already have a steady practice and like learning alone

    →

    Self-guided: Brasington, Burbea, Path to Nibbana

    Free or nearly free, and excellent. Come to a retreat if you stall or want feedback.

  7. I want a daily habit and less stress

    →

    A meditation app

    Cheap and low commitment. A retreat is more than you need for this.

  8. I want jhana within the Buddhist path, with a lineage teacher

    →

    A traditionally taught jhana retreat

    You get the full context and a teacher with decades of practice. Expect prerequisites.

  9. I'm dealing with trauma, depression or a crisis

    →

    Therapy first

    A meditation retreat is training, not treatment, and intensive practice can bring up hard material. Many alumni say therapy and jhana practice worked well together later on.

  10. I want one powerful experience, and I'm not after a skill

    →

    A legal, screened psilocybin session or retreat

    It works almost every time. Just know it's a visit, not something you learn to do.

In fairness

When Jhourney isn’t the right choice

Jhourney isn't the right choice for everyone. If you're in acute mental-health distress, start with therapy. We'll ask about your history, and some people will be asked to wait. If cost decides it, a free Vipassana course or a good book will teach you a lot. If lineage, ethics or the full Buddhist path matter to you, a traditional teacher will serve you better than we can. If you want relaxation or better sleep, an app is easier and cheaper. And if you need a guarantee, we can't give one: about 4 in 10 people don't reach jhana in the week. Many of them still tell us the retreat was worth it, but not all of them. Where we fit best is with someone who is reasonably stable, curious, willing to feel what comes up, and who wants to learn this specific skill fast with someone checking their work.

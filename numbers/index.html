We track everything. Including what doesn’t work.

01

About six in ten reach jhana in one week.

843 of 1,436 people with a known outcome reached jhana during their retreat, as judged by our staff. That number has barely moved in three years.

By year

  • 202458.4%n=493

By format

  • Online retreats57.5%n=757
  • In-person retreats60.1%n=679
  • Private retreats (≈4 students per teacher)71.8%n=110

Online works about as well as in person. The small private retreats do best, probably because each student gets far more teacher time.

02

Most get there by day three. Many don’t until later.

Of the people who reached jhana, half had by the end of Day 3. But 43% got there on Day 4 or after, which is why the retreat is a full week.

05

How good was the best moment?

We ask everyone: your best moment this week was as good as the best thing in your life over what period? 85% said a month or longer. 33% said a year or longer.

06

What went wrong.

Intensive meditation can stir things up. We log every adverse effect our team sees or hears about afterwards, and we publish the count.

07

What these numbers can’t tell you.

  1. Outcomes are judged by our staff, not measured by a device. Facilitators decide whether someone reached jhana using their descriptions, exit survey and interviews. Separately, 63.8% (n=1066) said on the exit survey that they had reached jhana.
  2. Not everyone is a first-timer. 127 of 1340 people came to more than one retreat, so 151 of the 1491 rows are a returning alum. Returning alums reach jhana more often (61.0% vs 58.2% on a first retreat), which nudges the overall rate up slightly.
  3. Results vary a lot between retreats and venues: from 35.0% to 77.3% across the 34 retreats with 20+ outcomes. Our two retreats at Zephyr Point came in lowest (42.0%, n=100), and we haven't fully explained why.
  4. Nobody, including us, can predict an individual's result from what they tell us before the retreat. The best prediction model we've built scored an F1 of about 0.59, barely better than guessing 'yes' for everyone. The predictors on this page shift the odds for groups; they don't decide anything for you.
  5. The predictor figures (prior joy, daily practice, occupation) come from a subset of participants who completed the detailed pre-retreat survey, so their sample sizes are smaller than the headline figures.
  6. Adverse effects are the cases our team logged, during the retreat or reported afterwards. Cases nobody told us about aren't counted, so treat the rate as a floor.
  7. Everyone with a missing answer is left out of that figure's denominator, which is why n differs from figure to figure.

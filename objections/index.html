Before you book

Still deciding? The honest answers to the doubts people have before booking

These are the questions people bring to our calls once they’re interested but not yet sure: will it work for me, will it last, and is it worth the money and the week off. Each one gets a straight answer, one number from our retreat data where we have one, and a link to the page that goes deeper. If the honest answer is that a retreat isn’t right for you yet, we say that too.

01

Will it work for me?

What happens if I’m in the unlucky 40%? Asked in ~33% of calls It happens. About 4 in 10 people don’t reach jhana in the week. Many still leave with something they value, but not all of them, and they rate the week lower.

We publish this because it’s the real risk. 58.7% of 1,436 participants reached jhana during their retreat, as judged by our staff. The rest didn’t, and nobody can tell in advance which group you’ll be in. That includes us: our best attempt to predict it from pre-retreat answers barely beat guessing.

What the others usually describe is the work that comes before jhana: feeling emotions more fully, a quieter inner critic, practising without grinding. Those skills don’t switch off when the week ends. But people who don’t reach jhana recommend us less (an NPS of 47, against 82 for those who do), and some leave disappointed.

If the only outcome you’d count is jhana itself, a retreat is a poor bet for you right now. If you’d value the skills along the way too, the odds look different.

I’ve barely meditated. Is this too advanced for me? Experience level comes up in ~15% of calls No. Retreats are built for beginners, and people with almost no practice reach jhana more than half the time.

Among people with 100 hours of meditation or less, 53.1% reached jhana during the retreat. Experience helps on average, but less than people assume, and plenty of participants have never been on a retreat before.

A few weeks of short daily sits beforehand seems to help more than years of occasional practice. People who meditated every day before their retreat reached jhana 71% of the time, against 51% for those who sat twice a week or less. That’s a correlation, not a promise, but it’s something you can start this week.

I’ve tried meditation before and it never really did anything for me. That’s common, and it isn’t your fault. Most meditation trains neutral attention with almost no feedback. This is a different practice, and you can test it tonight for free.

Most methods ask you to watch the breath and stay neutral. We start with a pleasant feeling, such as warmth, gratitude or a smile, and relax into it. It’s closer to a feeling practice than a focus practice, and people who bounced off breath meditation often find it suits them better.

The bigger difference is feedback. When a sit goes nowhere, a facilitator helps you work out why (trying too hard, watching the wrong sensation, steering around an emotion) and you change the experiment instead of repeating it for months.

You don’t need to take our word for any of this. The 25-minute sit every retreat opens with is free. If nothing happens, that’s useful information too.

Will it last?

Will it last once I’m home, or fade like every retreat high? Asked in ~39% of calls The afterglow fades, usually within days or weeks, and we tell everyone to expect that. What lasts is the skill, and it lasts the way any skill does: with practice.

Right after a retreat many people feel unusually light. In our 2024 alumni survey, that afterglow tended to fade after a few days to a few weeks, as ordinary life came back. That’s normal, and it isn’t the same as losing what you learned.

What alumni describe lasting is quieter: noticing emotions earlier, a kinder inner voice, and knowing from experience that good feeling can come from the inside. Access to jhana itself behaves like any skill. Some people keep it with a short regular practice; others let it slip and later have to rebuild it.

So we’d rather sell you a skill than a peak. The page below covers the three practice rhythms alumni actually use.

What share of people can reliably get back into jhana afterwards? About half of the people who reach jhana leave confident they can get back in, within an hour, whenever they want.

We call that deterministic access: being able to return to at least one jhana in under an hour when you choose to. 50.9% of those who reached jhana on retreat left with it. The rest have touched it, sometimes many times, but can’t yet bring it up on demand.

What happens next depends on practice. Some people build reliable access in the weeks after the retreat; some lose it for a while and get it back. Treat the week as the start of the skill rather than the finished thing.

Is it worth it?

Couldn’t I learn this myself, from books, an app or a free Vipassana course? Comparisons with Vipassana, apps or books come up in ~44% of calls Some people do, and if you have the time, instincts and patience, that may be your route. What a retreat adds is speed and feedback, not secret knowledge.

The jhanas are thousands of years old and the books are good. Our own full guide is free on this site. A 10-day Vipassana course costs nothing and is serious training, though it isn’t a jhana retreat.

The hard part of learning alone is that the mistakes happen inside your own head, where nobody can see them. People can sit for years without knowing whether they’re trying too hard or missing the feeling they’re meant to grow. A retreat compresses that: someone helps you see in a day what can take months to spot alone.

If you already have a steady practice and like learning on your own, start with the guide. Come to a retreat if you stall.

Why does a guided meditation cost $1,650 or more? Price, discounts or scholarships come up in ~31% of calls You’re not paying for the technique. That’s free in books. You’re paying for feedback: a week of someone watching your practice and helping you choose the next experiment.

Online retreats cost about $1,650. In person runs from around $4,000 to about $5,000 with room and meals, depending on venue and room. That’s real money, and we can’t guarantee a result.

Meditation has a guesswork problem. If you’re trying too hard, avoiding an emotion or watching the wrong sensation, nothing tells you, and you can repeat the mistake for months. The fee pays for daily 1:1s early in the week, two group sessions a day, and a curriculum built from watching more than a thousand people hit the same walls. A personal trainer is a closer comparison than an app.

If paying would mean debt, a drained emergency fund or real stress at home, don’t come yet. Ask about scholarships, or start with the free options.

Is the online retreat really as good as in person? Raised unprompted in ~18% of calls On results, close enough to call it even: 57.5% reached jhana online and 60.1% in person. The difference is the setting, not the teaching.

Both formats get the same curriculum, group sessions and 1:1s. In person, the venue handles rooms and meals and it’s easier to shut the world out. Online, you set up your own space, eat what you like and pay much less.

Some people find online is better practice for daily life, because you build the skill where you’ll use it. If your home is noisy or you know you’ll check email, in person is worth the extra.

The practicalities

A week of silence and 6–8 hours a day sounds like a lot. Questions about the shape of the day come up in ~72% of calls It’s less rigid than it sounds. The hours include group sessions, walking and lying down. There’s no 4 a.m. bell, and you can still talk in sessions and 1:1s.

About 3 hours a day are group sessions with instruction and Q&A, and mid-retreat most people log around 5 hours of practice in total. Silence means no casual chat; you can still ask questions, share in groups and talk with your facilitator. Napping is encouraged.

Because the practice is built on enjoying a feeling, sits often get longer on their own as the week goes on. The fears page has the longer answer, and the day-by-day page shows the actual schedule.

I can’t take a full week off work. Time off and work-compatible formats come up in ~39% of calls That’s what the work-compatible retreat is for: two Friday-to-Sunday retreat weekends with ordinary workdays in between. You take two Fridays off.

On the weekdays in between, you practise about 1.5 hours before work and again after. It costs the same as the full-time online retreat. It asks a lot of your evenings for about 11 days, but not a week of leave.

If you can take the week, full-time gives you more uninterrupted practice. If neither works right now, short daily sits are the best preparation for whenever you can.

I take medication or have a mental-health history. Can I come? Mental health, medication or harm comes up in ~25% of calls It depends on your history, which is why we ask about it before anyone books. Read our retreat policies, then talk it through with us on a call.

Intensive meditation can bring up difficult emotions, and the risk is greater for people with recent or unmanaged mental-health issues. Everyone shares their history before the retreat, and some people are asked to wait. We can’t advise on medication; that’s a conversation for your prescriber.

Should I wait until I’ve practised more, or until life calms down? Sometimes waiting is right. If money, health or work would make the week stressful, wait. If you’re waiting to feel ready, a short daily practice now is a better use of the time.

Many people think about this for a year or more before they book. There’s no deadline, and we’d rather you came when you can give the week your attention.

If you’re waiting, wait actively. People who meditated every day before their retreat reached jhana 71% of the time, against 51% for those who sat twice a week or less. Fifteen or twenty minutes a day is enough to start.

Good reasons to wait: financial strain, an acute crisis, or a week you know you can’t protect.

Will it change me in ways I don’t want?

If I’m happier, will I lose my edge? Comes up in a handful of calls It’s a fair worry, and not what we usually see. Alumni more often describe less fear behind their work, not less drive.

Founders and activists ask this a lot. What tends to change isn’t how much people care. It’s how much of their effort runs on anxiety. The practice teaches you to feel the difference between striving and wanting, and many people find they have more energy once less of it goes into fighting themselves.

Meditation can sharpen your sense of what you actually value, and occasionally people change plans because of it. Our advice is always the same: don’t make big life decisions straight after a retreat. Give them weeks or months to settle.

If it feels that good, couldn’t I get hooked? Comes up in a handful of calls Pleasure and addiction aren’t the same thing. Jhana is closer to the pleasure of a good friendship or a runner’s high than to a slot machine, and you can’t get in by craving it.

You can’t enter jhana by grabbing at it. The tension of wanting it badly is exactly what keeps people out, so the practice trains you to relax desire rather than feed it. That runs the opposite way to a compulsive habit.

Alumni describe something closer to the reverse of addiction. Knowing they can bring good feeling back on their own loosens the sense that it’s scarce. Most people end up using jhana as a tool, not as somewhere to live.

On a call“You can’t crave your way in. The practice trains you to relax wanting, which is the opposite of addiction. Alumni describe good feeling seeming less scarce, not more chasing.”

Is this religious? Do I have to believe anything? No. We teach the jhanas as a secular, trainable skill. There’s no guru, no belief system and no ritual you have to buy into.

The jhanas were mapped in Buddhist texts about 2,500 years ago, and contemplatives in other traditions describe similar states. We didn’t invent them and don’t claim to. We teach them in plain language: feelings in the body, relaxation, curiosity and experiments.

If lineage, ethics or the full Buddhist path matter to you, a traditional teacher will serve you better, and we’ll say so. The fears page answers both versions of this worry: too woo, and not woo enough.

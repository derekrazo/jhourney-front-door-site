My Path to Jhana

What’s most likely to get in your way?

Fourteen questions, about four minutes. This quiz won't tell you whether you'll reach jhana. Nothing we've built can predict that for one person, and we've tried. What it can do is point to the habit most likely to trip you up, show where that habit tends to appear on the Jhana Map, and give you something to practise this week. Answer for how things usually are, not how you'd like them to be. There are no good or bad results.

Your result

The Playful Explorer

Friendly starting conditions. Your job is to keep them light.

You tend to meet obstacles with curiosity, feel emotions in your body without much effort, and go easy on yourself when things go wrong. Those habits overlap with what our facilitators notice in students who settle in quickly. It isn't a promise: people with answers like yours still hit walls, often right after an early high.

What you bring

  • You treat problems as experiments
  • Emotions show up in your body without much digging
  • Mistakes don't send you into a spiral
  • You recover perspective quickly

What may trip you up

  • Chasing an early high instead of the skills that produced it
  • Assuming you've understood the instructions when you've missed something subtle
  • Holding the wheel when a feeling gets bigger than you expected

Five things to practise

  1. 01

    Hold early wins lightly

    A quick start is a good sign, not a guarantee. The common stall for people like you comes after a great sit, when every later sit becomes an attempt to get it back.

    Try thisAfter any standout sit, write down what you did. Then start the next one as if it were your first day.

  2. 02

    Ask what you're getting wrong

    Confidence and ownership help. Certainty that you've understood everything doesn't. Even capable students are usually making a mistake they can't see from the inside.

    Try thisBefore each sit, ask 'What am I most likely getting wrong?' and run one small experiment on the answer.

  3. 03

    Let it get big

    Confident people are more likely to steer their experience without noticing. Sometimes the useful instruction isn't curiosity but surrender: let the feeling grow until it overwhelms you.

    Try thisWhen a good feeling grows, drop your expectations about what should happen next, as if you were a newborn with no map at all.

  4. 04

    Sit longer than you need to

    Many meditators report settling in at around 45 minutes. Past that point, a little extra time buys a lot of high-quality minutes.

    Try thisAdd 15 minutes to your usual sit and spend them simply enjoying whatever is there.

  5. 05

    Practise daily now

    Students who meditated every day before their retreat reached jhana 71% of the time, against 51% for those who practised twice a week or less. That's a correlation, but it costs little to act on.

    Try thisFor the next two weeks, do 20 minutes a day of the loop: find what feels good, sink into it, get curious about it.

The Over-Efforter

Effort got you this far. Here, it's the thing to dial down.

You hold yourself to a high standard, and when something goes wrong your first feeling is often shame or frustration. That drive has served you well. In this practice, though, extra effort and self-criticism add exactly the tension you're trying to release. Over-efforting is the most common challenge our facilitators write down, and easing off is one of the changes that most often helps.

What you bring

  • Persistence when things are hard
  • High standards and real motivation
  • Willingness to put in the hours
  • You take the practice seriously

What may trip you up

  • Pressing the button harder: repeating the phrase faster when nothing happens
  • Turning each sit into a performance review
  • Reaching for jhana when it feels close, and pushing it away

Also worth watching:

Five things to practise

  1. 01

    Cut your effort in half, twice

    Unless you're unusually good at relaxing, the effort you plan to bring is too much. Most students discover they've been tensing in ways they didn't know about.

    Try thisIn your next sit, use half the effort you normally would. Then halve it again, and notice what happens to the feeling.

  2. 02

    Make enjoyment the scoreboard

    If enjoyment, curiosity or ease aren't showing up on their own, that isn't a signal to try harder. It's a signal to change what you're doing.

    Try thisRate each sit only on how much you enjoyed it, and drop any rating of what you achieved.

  3. 03

    Meet the critic with kindness

    Self-criticism can be faster than thought, and it recurses: you criticise yourself for being critical. The way out is to be kind all the way down. If you can't love the feeling, love the resistance; if you can't love that, love the resistance to that.

    Try thisWhen the critic shows up, look at it the way you'd look at a frustrated child, and wish it well.

  4. 04

    Let a small thing be enough

    Strivers often dismiss a faint warm feeling because it isn't impressive. That faint feeling is exactly what grows.

    Try thisCount a 2-out-of-10 warmth as a success and simply rest there for a minute.

  5. 05

    Try forgiveness practice

    Forgiveness is how our retreats usually work with self-criticism, and it pairs well with gratitude. You don't need a specific thing to forgive; repeating the phrases with soft attention is enough to start.

    Try thisFor ten minutes, gently repeat 'I forgive myself for not understanding' and notice what comes up.

The Sense-Maker

You understand first and feel second. This practice asks for the reverse.

You like to understand what's happening before you trust it, and when an emotion shows up you tend to explain it. That serves you well in most of life. In jhana practice, the analysis tends to keep running in the background and holds you one step away from the feeling itself.

What you bring

  • Precise, honest reports of what you experience
  • Careful, structured experiments
  • You read instructions closely
  • You notice details other people miss

What may trip you up

  • Narrating and evaluating the sit while it happens
  • Needing to know it's 'right' before letting a feeling grow
  • Checking whether a new state is 'it', which interrupts it

Also worth watching:

Five things to practise

  1. 01

    Feel now, analyse later

    Your analysis is useful. It just belongs after the sit, not during it.

    Try thisKeep a notebook beside you. During the sit, no thinking about the sit. All analysis goes in the notebook afterwards.

  2. 02

    Soft awareness over sharp focus

    A sharp, watchful mind adds tension. One student's breakthrough came from imagining brushing their experience with kindness instead of examining it.

    Try thisFor one sit, imagine you're stroking the feeling with tenderness rather than looking at it.

  3. 03

    Put your thinking where it helps

    There are endless decisions in meditation, and some structure helps. Use your planning mind before and after the sit, so it can rest during.

    Try thisBefore a sit, pick one experiment. Afterwards, write one thing you learned. Then stop thinking about it until tomorrow.

  4. 04

    Practise not knowing

    Assuming you already know what a feeling is like makes it less interesting. Curiosity grows when you drop that certainty.

    Try thisFor five minutes, explore a pleasant feeling as if you'd never met it: its texture, edges, temperature.

  5. 05

    Check what the checking protects

    A need to understand first is often a way of staying safe from failing or from feeling too much. Seeing that tends to loosen it.

    Try thisWhen you catch yourself checking, ask 'What am I afraid would happen if I stopped?' and welcome whatever answer comes.

The Mover

When it gets uncomfortable, you move. The practice is staying a little longer.

When you're bored, lonely or uneasy, your instinct is to do something: check your phone, start a task, change the channel. Almost everyone does this to some degree. A retreat removes most of those exits, so restlessness that was always there gets louder at first, and learning to stay with it is a big part of the work.

What you bring

  • Energy and appetite for new experiences
  • Quick to try things
  • Easy to engage and get interested
  • You don't stay stuck in a rut for long

What may trip you up

  • Ending sits early when restlessness shows up
  • Switching phrase or technique as soon as one goes flat
  • Keeping your phone nearby and losing whole days to it

Also worth watching:

Five things to practise

  1. 01

    Stay three breaths longer

    The urge to get up is usually a wave, not a wall. Staying through it a few times teaches your system that it passes.

    Try thisWhen you want to end a sit, stay for three more breaths and get curious about the urge itself.

  2. 02

    Make boredom interesting

    Restlessness is a form of tension you haven't welcomed yet. Running from it keeps it going; getting curious about it often dissolves it.

    Try thisNext time you're bored, find where boredom lives in your body and describe it in three words.

  3. 03

    Pick one and stay

    When a phrase goes flat, the novelty has worn off. The lesson is in how you're now relating to it, not in finding a fresher one.

    Try thisChoose one phrase or image for a whole week. Keep it fresh by changing how you hold it, not what it is.

  4. 04

    Put the exits out of reach

    We've had retreat students regret keeping their devices: 'I lost two days on this retreat because I didn't turn off my device.'

    Try thisPractise with your phone in another room this week, and plan to hand it in on retreat.

  5. 05

    Use movement, not escape

    You don't have to sit still for hours. On retreat, beginners often sit 30 minutes, walk slowly for 15, then sit again.

    Try thisSit for 30 minutes, do 15 minutes of slow walking while keeping the feeling going, then sit again.

The Even Keel

Steady on the surface, with feelings turned down low.

You're steady. Strong emotions rarely knock you over, and you may find it hard to say where in your body a feeling lives, or to remember the last time you cried. Jhana practice works with emotion, so your early work is gently turning up the volume on feelings that are already there.

What you bring

  • Calm under pressure
  • Not easily overwhelmed
  • Comfortable with long, quiet sits
  • A stable base to build on

What may trip you up

  • Finding no feeling to work with at first
  • Chasing physical tingles with no emotional tone
  • The belief that 'I just don't feel much'

Also worth watching:

Five things to practise

  1. 01

    Lower the bar

    You're probably looking for something bigger than what's there. A faint warmth, or even 'not unpleasant', is a real starting point.

    Try thisLook for anything that's slightly pleasant and rest your attention there for one minute, without trying to make it bigger.

  2. 02

    Start with whatever is easiest to feel

    Some people feel more through sadness, or in moments with other people, than through joy on its own. Any feeling counts as a way in.

    Try thisRecall a time someone made you laugh while things were hard, and notice where that lands in your body.

  3. 03

    Catch the quiet ones

    Mad, sad, glad and scared all show up in the body, often at a low volume you've learned to skip. The emotion you've felt least recently is probably your most guarded one.

    Try thisPick the one of the four you've felt least in the last day, and this week try to notice it early, at a 2 out of 10.

  4. 04

    Relax before you look

    For people who rarely cry, we usually start with deep physical and mental relaxation, then forgiveness practice. You don't need to cry to reach jhana, but you do need some emotional fluidity.

    Try thisSpend ten minutes softening your jaw, chest and belly before you look for any feeling at all.

  5. 05

    Question the belief

    Students who dropped the belief that they couldn't feel emotions, and the expectation that the feeling had to be big, often found more there than they expected.

    Try thisWrite 'I don't feel much', then list three moments this month when you felt something, however small.

Your result

The Protector

Feelings rise easily. Then something in you hits the brakes.

Feelings come up for you, sometimes quickly. When they start to get big, something in you tightens, pulls back or goes numb. That reflex is protective and it's there for good reasons. Jhana grows when a good feeling is allowed to get bigger than is comfortable, so your work is widening the range you can stay present for, at your own pace.

What you bring

  • Real access to emotion
  • Sensitivity to subtle shifts
  • A strong instinct for safety
  • Feelings move when you let them

What may trip you up

  • Bracing as the feeling builds
  • Swinging between too much and shutting down
  • Steering around particular emotions that feel unsafe

Also worth watching:

Five things to practise

  1. 01

    Lean in, lean back

    You don't have to leap. Go a little toward the intensity, come back to safety, and repeat. Each round tends to widen what you can hold.

    Try thisLet a good feeling grow until the first hint of tightening, then rest in something steady, like your feet on the floor, before leaning in again.

  2. 02

    Add a phrase of safety

    Letting go can feel risky. Some students balance it with phrases that remind them they're safe.

    Try thisAdd 'I'm safe' or 'this is allowed' to your phrase of kind wishes, and notice whether the feeling can grow a little more.

  3. 03

    Be kind to the brace

    Fighting the tightening adds more tension. Welcoming it often lets it soften on its own.

    Try thisWhen you tighten, wish the tightening well, as if it were a part of you trying to help.

  4. 04

    Know your range

    If you feel flooded, slow down and ground yourself. If you feel numb or far away, add a little energy. Both are normal, and both are information.

    Try thisIf flooded, open your eyes and name five things you can see. If numb, sit up, move a little, or take a few fuller breaths.

  5. 05

    You don't have to clear everything

    You don't need to process your whole emotional backlog to practise jhana, only what's stopping you from sinking in. If something heavy keeps coming up, getting support from a therapist alongside your practice is a strength, not a detour.

    Try thisFor ten minutes, gently repeat 'I forgive myself for not understanding' and stop if it feels like too much.
